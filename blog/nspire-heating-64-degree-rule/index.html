NSPIRE's 64°F Heating Rule:
What Every Section 8 Landlord Must Know Before October 1

Between October 1 and March 31, your Section 8 unit must maintain at least 64°F using a permanently installed heating source. Below that threshold is a Life-Threatening defect. Between 64°F and 67.9°F is Severe. Either one fails your HCV inspection — and the clock starts the moment the inspector reads the thermometer.

By Perfect Tenant Innovation  ·  September 2026  ·  9 min read

Source: HUD NSPIRE HCV/PBV Inspection Checklist (OMB 2577-0289) · 24 CFR 5.703 · HUD REAC NSPIRE Standards

The rule Temperature thresholds What counts as heating All heating deficiencies Real scenarios Action steps

October 1 is the date that every Section 8 landlord needs to have circled on their calendar — not because of rent, not because of lease renewals, but because of thermometers. Under NSPIRE, the first day of October marks the beginning of the heating season window, and from that day through March 31, the temperature inside every HCV unit you own must be maintained at a minimum of 64°F using a permanently installed heating source.

If an NSPIRE inspector arrives at your unit on any day between October 1 and March 31 and the thermometer reads below 64°F, your unit has a Life-Threatening defect. If it reads between 64°F and 67.9°F, your unit has a Severe defect. Both are fails. Both require correction. And the only temperature that clears the heating standard without triggering any deficiency is 68°F or above.

This is not a standard that existed under UPCS in the same form. UPCS required functional heating. NSPIRE requires functional heating and a specific measurable temperature outcome. The difference is the difference between "your furnace turns on" and "your furnace maintains 68 degrees when the inspector puts a thermometer in the room." Those are not the same standard, and landlords who managed to the old one without ever checking actual temperatures are at significant risk under the new one.

The heating season rule — exactly as written in the NSPIRE checklist

The NSPIRE HCV Inspection Checklist (OMB 2577-0289) contains three separate HVAC deficiency conditions related to temperature during the heating season. Understanding each one precisely is essential because they are graded differently and require different responses.

🌡️ Heating season — active window
October 1 – March 31
Temperature requirements apply. Below 64°F = Life-Threatening. 64–67.9°F = Severe. Permanently installed heating source must be present, operational, and maintaining temperature.
☀️ Off-season — April 1 through September 30
April 1 – September 30
Different rule applies. A permanently installed heating source that is damaged, inoperable, missing, or not installed during this window is a Moderate defect — 30-day fail. The heating source must exist and be capable of functioning, even when not in active use.
The off-season Moderate defect catches landlords off-guard A broken furnace discovered during a summer inspection is still a Moderate fail under NSPIRE — even though the temperature threshold doesn't apply. The heating source must be present and functional year-round. A landlord who defers furnace repair from April through September and gets inspected in July still fails NSPIRE on this item. The heating season window changes the severity — not the requirement.

The gap between 64°F and 68°F is the zone that will trap the most landlords. A working furnace that is slightly undersized, insufficiently insulated, or running on a degraded heat exchanger may maintain 65°F or 66°F consistently — technically keeping the unit warm enough to be habitable — while still producing a Severe NSPIRE fail. "My furnace is running" is no longer the standard. "My furnace is maintaining 68°F or above" is the standard.

What counts as a permanently installed heating source — and what doesn't

The NSPIRE heating requirement is not just about temperature. It specifies that the temperature must be maintained by a permanently installed heating source. That distinction matters because it determines whether a landlord's response to a cold unit — plugging in a space heater — is a solution or a compounding problem.

✓ Qualifies as permanently installed
Heating sources that satisfy NSPIRE
  • Central forced-air HVAC system (gas or electric)
  • Electric baseboard heaters permanently wired to the unit
  • Radiator systems connected to a building-wide boiler
  • Heat pump systems permanently installed
  • Wall-mounted electric heaters permanently wired
  • Hydronic radiant floor heating systems
✗ Does not qualify — and may cause additional fails
Heating sources that do NOT satisfy NSPIRE
  • Portable electric space heaters
  • Portable oil-filled radiators
  • Unvented gas space heaters (also a separate LT fail)
  • Unvented kerosene heaters (also a separate LT fail)
  • Wood-burning stoves not connected to a permanent system
  • Plug-in ceramic tile heaters
A portable space heater doesn't fix a heating fail — it adds one If a Section 8 landlord places an unvented gas or kerosene space heater in a unit to compensate for a broken furnace, they have not fixed the NSPIRE fail. They have added a second Life-Threatening defect — unvented heaters that burn gas, oil, or kerosene are independently classified as Life-Threatening under NSPIRE regardless of the season. One broken furnace becomes two 24-hour Life-Threatening fails. The only fix is repairing or replacing the permanently installed heating system.

Every NSPIRE heating deficiency — complete reference

The NSPIRE checklist contains more heating-related deficiency conditions than most landlords realize. Temperature is only part of the picture. Here is every HVAC deficiency on the HCV checklist, severity-rated:

Deficiency condition Severity Timeframe
Inspection date Oct 1–Mar 31 AND permanently installed heating source is not working OR interior temperature is below 64°F Life-Threatening 24 hours
Unvented space heater that burns gas, oil, or kerosene is present Life-Threatening 24 hours
Combustion chamber cover or gas shutoff valve is missing from a fuel-burning heating appliance Life-Threatening 24 hours
Fuel-burning heating system exhaust vent is misaligned, blocked, disconnected, improperly connected, damaged, or missing Life-Threatening 24 hours
Inspection date Oct 1–Mar 31 AND heating source is working AND interior temperature is 64°F to 67.9°F Severe 30 days
Heating system or device safety shield is damaged or missing Severe 30 days
Air conditioning system or device is not operational Moderate 30 days
Inspection date Oct 1–Mar 31 AND permanently installed heating source is inoperable Moderate 30 days
Inspection date Apr 1–Sep 30 AND permanently installed heating source is damaged, inoperable, missing, or not installed Moderate 30 days

Four separate heating-related conditions trigger a Life-Threatening fail. Landlords who focus only on the 64°F temperature threshold and overlook exhaust vent alignment, combustion chamber covers, or unvented supplemental heaters are leaving themselves exposed to additional 24-hour correction requirements they may not have anticipated.

Real scenarios: how these rules play out in practice

🌡️
Scenario 1: Inspector arrives November 15. Furnace is running. Thermometer reads 61°F.
The furnace is operational — it's working, just not keeping up. Doesn't matter. The interior temperature is below 64°F during heating season. This is a Life-Threatening defect under the first HVAC deficiency condition. The landlord has 24 hours to bring the temperature above 64°F and notify the PHA. If the furnace can't maintain 64°F, it needs to be repaired or replaced within that window.
Life-Threatening · 24-hour correction
🌡️
Scenario 2: Inspector arrives February 8. Furnace is running. Thermometer reads 66°F.
The furnace is functional and the unit is genuinely warm — but 66°F falls in the 64–67.9°F Severe band. This is a Severe defect, not a Life-Threatening one, so the correction window is 30 days rather than 24 hours. But it is still a fail. The landlord needs to diagnose why the system can't reach 68°F — undersized unit, poor insulation, degraded heat exchanger — and correct it within 30 days.
Severe · 30-day correction
🔥
Scenario 3: Inspector arrives January 20. Furnace is broken. Tenant is using a kerosene space heater. Unit reads 70°F.
The temperature itself passes — 70°F is above 68°F. But the landlord now has two Life-Threatening defects: (1) the permanently installed heating source is not working during heating season, which triggers the first HVAC deficiency; and (2) an unvented kerosene heater is present in the unit, which is independently Life-Threatening. The tenant's workaround made the inspection worse, not better. Two 24-hour correction clocks are now running.
Two Life-Threatening fails · 24-hour correction each
✅
Scenario 4: Inspector arrives December 5. Furnace serviced September 15. Thermometer reads 71°F.
The furnace was professionally serviced six weeks before heating season began. It is operational, maintaining temperature above 68°F, no unvented supplemental heaters are present, the exhaust vent is intact, and the combustion chamber cover is in place. Every heating-related NSPIRE item passes. This is the outcome September HVAC maintenance is designed to produce.
Pass — no heating deficiencies recorded

Action steps: what to do before October 1 every year

1
Schedule professional HVAC service for every unit in September — not when a problem is reported
A September HVAC service call — before October 1 — is now standard NSPIRE compliance practice, not optional maintenance. Have a technician inspect the heat exchanger, test the system's ability to maintain temperature, check the exhaust vent alignment, and confirm the combustion chamber cover is in place. Get the service documented in writing. That documentation is your evidence if the PHA questions your compliance.
2
Test the actual interior temperature — not just whether the furnace turns on
After your HVAC service, set the thermostat to a heating demand temperature and measure actual interior temperature with a thermometer — not just confirm that the unit blows warm air. A furnace that runs but only delivers 65°F will fail NSPIRE's Severe threshold even though it appears functional. Know your actual temperature output before the inspector does.
3
Walk every unit before October 1 and remove any unvented supplemental heaters
Walk every HCV unit before October 1. If a tenant has brought in a portable gas, oil, or kerosene heater — for any reason — it must be removed before an inspection can occur. An unvented fuel-burning heater is a Life-Threatening fail independent of the temperature in the unit. A unit at 75°F with an unvented kerosene heater still fails.
4
Address insulation and weatherization deficiencies that prevent temperature maintenance
A furnace that runs correctly but can't maintain 68°F in a poorly insulated unit is a NSPIRE fail that can't be solved by HVAC repair alone. If your units have significant air leakage, inadequate insulation, or drafty windows, those physical deficiencies will produce temperature readings in the Severe range even with a properly functioning furnace. Weatherization is now a NSPIRE compliance issue, not just an energy efficiency one.
5
Create a written heating season compliance protocol and share it with tenants
Give tenants a written note before October 1: who to call if heat is not working, what your response time commitment is, and that portable gas or kerosene heaters are prohibited in the unit. A tenant who understands the rules is a compliance partner. A tenant who doesn't may introduce an unvented heater that creates a second Life-Threatening fail on your next inspection.
September HVAC service is the single most effective pre-inspection investment a Section 8 landlord can make A professional HVAC service call before October 1 typically costs $80–$200 per unit. It confirms your system can maintain 68°F, catches exhaust vent misalignment before it becomes a Life-Threatening fail, documents your proactive compliance, and eliminates the two most consequential NSPIRE heating deficiencies simultaneously. One service call. Four Life-Threatening risks addressed. The math is straightforward.
