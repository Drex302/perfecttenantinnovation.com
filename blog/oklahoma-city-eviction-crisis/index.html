Oklahoma City: A $58 Filing, a $33 Eviction, and the Fastest Removal in America

One recent Oklahoma County case sought $33 in back rent. In a state with one of the nation's fastest eviction timelines and $58 filing fees, that's all it takes. This is the last city in our wave — and it holds all the others.

The last city, and the sum of all of them Over seventeen cities, this wave has isolated the parts of the American eviction machine one at a time: cheap filing fees in Kansas City, permanent records in Houston, corporate landlords in Tampa, landlord-tenant power imbalance everywhere. Oklahoma City is where every one of those parts operates at once, in the same courthouse, at the same time — and where the data documenting it is among the freshest and most complete in the country. If you wanted to build the most efficient eviction system in America, you would build Oklahoma.

The summation city

We saved Oklahoma City for last on purpose. It isn't the highest-profile city in this series, and its historic Black district isn't the most famous. But no single city better assembles every mechanism the wave has documented into one working system. A landlord in Oklahoma County operates with the cheapest filing fees, one of the fastest timelines, permanently public records, a courtroom tilted overwhelmingly in their favor, and a data trail showing they use all of it — not as a last resort, but as a routine way to collect rent. Oklahoma City is the eviction crisis with nothing hidden.

The eviction numbers

12%
Oklahoma County's 2025 eviction filing rate — 12 filings per 100 renter households, more than double New York City's rate, and doubled over the past decade
70% vs 5%
Share of landlords with legal counsel in OKC eviction court (70%) versus tenants (5%) — one of the starkest power imbalances documented in this wave

Oklahoma City's eviction rate has doubled since 2016, when Eviction Lab first ranked it among the 20 highest-evicting cities in the nation. Today it sits at roughly 12% — more than double New York City's — according to a new dataset compiled by Eviction Lab in collaboration with Mental Health Association Oklahoma and Oklahoma City University's data-analytics center, which now updates OKC's numbers monthly. The disparities inside that number are, by now, familiar to any reader of this series: Black renters are 22% of Oklahoma City's renter population but 37% of eviction filings. Women make up 46% of filings. And more than 40% of all eviction filings in Oklahoma County come from just 100 properties — the same landlord-concentration pattern this wave found in every city that had the data to show it.

The $33 case Oklahoma Watch found that over one recent year, 37% of Oklahoma County eviction claims were for less than $1,000, and half were for less than the median monthly rent. One case filed in Oklahoma County sought $33 in past-due rent — and was dismissed the same day it was filed. When filing costs a landlord $58 and takes minutes, even $33 is worth pursuing in court. The filing itself — the fees, the record, the threat — does the work, whether or not the case ever has merit.

Every mechanism at once

Here is what makes Oklahoma City the summation of this wave. Read down this list, and you'll recognize every city that came before it:

The lowest filing fee yet — $58. Even cheaper than Kansas City's $62. Eviction Lab's own research says raising the fee would cut filings and help Black renters most. (see: Kansas City)
One of the fastest timelines in the nation. Nearly 40% of evicted tenants were locked out within 14 days of a filing in 2025 — some in as few as seven days. (the speed Houston's advocates warned about)
Permanent public records. In Oklahoma, most eviction records remain publicly available indefinitely — the filing follows a tenant forever, exactly as in Texas. (see: Houston)
Concentration among a few landlords. 40%+ of filings from 100 properties — including out-of-state corporate operators and mobile-home-park owners running serial evictions. (see: Tampa, Houston)
A staggering courtroom power imbalance. 70% of landlords have a lawyer; 5% of tenants do — the mirror image of Kansas City's right-to-counsel win, without the win. (the gap KC organized to close)
~$180 in fees per filing. Eviction Lab estimates each filing adds about $180 in fines and fees for a typical renter — a tax stacked on the households least able to absorb it. (see: Phoenix, everywhere)

The veto

If Kansas City is this wave's proof that policy can change, Oklahoma City is its reminder of how hard that change can be to win. In 2025, the Oklahoma Legislature considered a bill to modestly extend the state's eviction timeline — to give tenants a few more days before removal. Governor Kevin Stitt vetoed it, arguing it would make eviction "more burdensome" for landlords and might discourage them from renting to low-income households at all. Housing analysts noted that the veto message ran contrary to the data: Oklahoma already has one of the shortest eviction timelines in the country, and lengthening it is among the clearest levers available to reduce the state's sixth-highest-in-the-nation eviction rate.

The veto is a clean illustration of the power imbalance the courtroom numbers already imply. Even the most modest structural fix — a handful of extra days — met a wall. That is the environment Oklahoma City tenants navigate, and the reason a tool that works outside the legislature, one household at a time, matters so much here.

Deep Deuce

Every city in this wave has a Black neighborhood that thrived under segregation and was later dismantled. Oklahoma City's is Deep Deuce — and its story closes the wave's historical arc almost too perfectly.

1920s–1950s

Centered on NE 2nd Street, Deep Deuce is the regional center of Black jazz, culture, and commerce — "similar to Harlem in the 1930s." It produces Ralph Ellison (author of Invisible Man), jazz greats Charlie Christian and Jimmy Rushing, and Roscoe Dunjee's pioneering civil-rights newspaper, the Black Dispatch.

1960s–1980s

Urban renewal — what a local official bluntly calls "urban removal" — and the construction of Interstate 235 cut directly through northeast OKC. The Oklahoma DOT buys up most of Deep Deuce's parcels; the community is severed and dispersed.

2001–today

Deep Deuce is redeveloped into "trendy restaurants, apartments, and high-end brownstones." The name — now trademarked — is attached to a luxury apartment complex built on the site of the community that was displaced.

Sit with that last detail, because it is the sharpest version of a pattern this wave has traced from Tampa's Scrub to Columbus's Million Dollar Mile to St. Louis's Ville. Deep Deuce was a Black neighborhood erased by a highway and urban renewal. What stands there now is a set of luxury apartments that use its name — the culture kept as a brand, the community kept out by the rent. An Oklahoma City Urban Renewal official said it plainly: the program "disrupted places where people grew up, where their grandparents lived, where they went to church," and today the agency works "to try and repair some of that damage."

"Urban renewal was also known as urban removal." — a description of what happened to Deep Deuce, echoing the same phrase Black residents used in nearly every city in this wave

Where PTI fits

Oklahoma City is the summation of the problem, so it's a fitting place to state the summation of the response. Perfect Tenant Innovation can't lower the $58 fee, speed-limit the timeline, seal the records, or override a governor's veto — those are the structural fights, and they are worth fighting. What PTI changes is the one thing all of those mechanisms depend on: the moment a shortfall becomes a filing. By rewarding on-time payment, building a tenant's credit through rent, and giving landlords a documented reason to accept a payment plan instead of reaching for a $58 filing, PTI intervenes before the fastest eviction system in the country ever gets to start its clock.

In a courtroom where 70% of landlords have a lawyer and 5% of tenants do, the best defense is never entering the courtroom at all. That's the household-level fix this whole wave has been building toward — and Oklahoma City, holding all the others inside it, is exactly where it's needed most.
