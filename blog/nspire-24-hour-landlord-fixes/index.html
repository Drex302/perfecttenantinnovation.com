Your Landlord Has 24 Hours to Fix These Problems.
Here's the Full List.

HUD's NSPIRE standards call the most dangerous problems in a home "Life-Threatening." When an inspection finds one, your landlord has 24 hours to fix it. Not 24 days. Here's what counts, and what to do if you find one where you live.

By Perfect Tenant Innovation  ·  September 2026  ·  7 min read

Source: HUD NSPIRE HCV Inspection Checklist (OMB 2577-0289) · HUD REAC NSPIRE Standards · 24 CFR 982

🚨 Some of these can't wait for any deadline. Act now if:
  • You smell gas. Leave the home right away. Don't flip switches or light anything. From outside, call 911 or your gas company's emergency line.
  • A carbon monoxide alarm is sounding, or people in the home feel dizzy, sick, or confused. Get everyone outside and call 911.
  • Water is touching wiring, outlets, or a light fixture. Stay away from it and don't touch anything electrical nearby.
  • There's fire, smoke, or sparking. Get out and call 911.

In Post 1 of this series, we covered the big picture: HUD's new NSPIRE inspection standard puts your health and safety first. This post focuses on the most important part of that promise, the problems so dangerous that your landlord gets just 24 hours to fix them after an inspection finds them.

NSPIRE calls these "Life-Threatening" defects. They're the problems most likely to hurt someone quickly, through fire, electrocution, poisoning, or extreme cold. Knowing the list helps you recognize when something in your home is more than an inconvenience, and it tells you when to push hard for a fast fix.

The 24-hour list: what NSPIRE calls Life-Threatening

🔥
Missing or broken smoke alarm
A smoke alarm that is missing or doesn't sound when tested.
Check: press the test button. You should hear a loud beep.
☠️
Carbon monoxide alarm problems
A CO alarm that is missing where one is required, blocked, or doesn't sound when tested. This is new under NSPIRE.
Check: homes with gas appliances or an attached garage usually need one near the bedrooms.
🥶
No heat in winter
From October 1 to March 31: the heating system isn't working, or the inside temperature is below 64°F.
Check: a cheap thermometer tells you the real temperature.
⚡
Exposed electrical wiring
Bare or loose wires you can see, like an open junction box or wires sticking out of a wall or ceiling.
Check: never touch it. Take a photo from a safe distance.
💧
Water touching electrical parts
A leak dripping into a light fixture, an outlet, or the electrical panel.
Check: look for water stains around ceiling lights under a bathroom.
⛽
Gas or fuel leak
A natural gas, propane, or oil leak from an appliance, line, or tank.
Check: a rotten-egg smell means leave now and call from outside.
🚪
Blocked way out
An exit path you can't use in an emergency, such as a door or escape route that's blocked or won't open.
Check: can everyone get out quickly if there's a fire?
🌫️
Blocked or loose heater vent
The exhaust vent on a gas furnace or water heater is blocked, disconnected, or damaged, or safety parts are missing.
Check: the metal pipe on top of the furnace or water heater should be connected and intact.
🔥
Unvented fuel-burning heater
A gas, propane, oil, or kerosene space heater with no vent to the outside, even if it's turned off.
Check: electric space heaters are not in this category.
🍄
Extreme mold inside your unit
Mold at extremely high levels, such as heavy growth spreading across walls or ceilings.
Check: smaller amounts of mold still count. Those are 30-day fixes, covered in Post 3.
Not on this list doesn't mean not serious Plenty of real problems, like a broken lock, a pest infestation, a leaking pipe, or a broken outlet, are 30-day fixes rather than 24-hour fixes. They still count, and your landlord still has to fix them. The next post in this series covers that list.

How the 24-hour clock works

It helps to understand exactly when the 24 hours applies. The deadline starts when an inspection by your Public Housing Authority (PHA) documents the problem. That's either your regular inspection, or a special inspection you ask for when something goes wrong between regular visits.

From problem to fix
1. You find it
Document it and report it to your landlord in writing right away.
2. Inspection
A PHA inspector confirms it's Life-Threatening.
3. 24 hours
Your landlord must fix it and tell the PHA.
4. If not
The PHA can stop paying the landlord and act to protect you.

You don't have to wait for your annual inspection. If a dangerous problem shows up between inspections and your landlord doesn't fix it quickly, most PHAs will send an inspector when a tenant reports a serious problem. Once that inspector confirms a Life-Threatening defect, the 24-hour clock applies.

What to do if you find one of these in your home

1
Get safe first
For gas smells, a sounding CO alarm, fire, or water on wiring, leave or keep your distance and call 911 or your utility company. Paperwork can wait until everyone is safe.
2
Take dated photos or video
Capture the problem clearly, from a safe distance. For heat, photograph a thermometer showing the temperature. Your phone records the date and time automatically, and that record matters.
3
Tell your landlord in writing, the same day
Send a text, email, or message through a tenant platform. Say what the problem is, where it is, when you noticed it, and that it's a safety emergency. A phone call is fine too, but follow it with something in writing so there's a record.
4
Call your Housing Authority if it isn't fixed fast
If your landlord doesn't respond or fix it promptly, contact your PHA, explain the problem, and ask for an inspection. Have your photos and your written report ready. Your PHA administers your voucher and enforces these standards.
5
Keep everything
Save your messages, photos, the date the inspector came, and the date the fix was made. If there's ever a dispute about what happened or when, your records speak for you.

A message you can send your landlord

📋 Copy, fill in, and send
Hi [Landlord name],

I'm reporting a safety problem at [address, unit #]. As of [date and time], [describe the problem, for example: "the heat is not working and the temperature inside is 58°F" or "the carbon monoxide alarm does not sound when I press the test button"].

This is a Life-Threatening condition under HUD's NSPIRE standards and needs to be fixed right away. I've attached photos. Please let me know today when it will be repaired.

Thank you,
[Your name] · [Phone]

Your part: a few things are the tenant's responsibility

It's worth being honest about one thing. Under Housing Choice Voucher rules, families are responsible for problems they cause. That includes some items on this list.

Avoid creating a 24-hour problem yourself Don't remove smoke or CO alarm batteries or take alarms down, even if they chirp or go off while you cook. Report a faulty alarm instead. Don't bring in a gas, propane, or kerosene space heater. If your home is too cold, report it to your landlord. That's their job to fix, and an electric heater is the safer stopgap. Keep exits clear of furniture and storage. Problems a household causes can be charged to that household and can put the family's assistance at risk.
The bottom line If your home has a Life-Threatening problem, you have a right to a fast fix, and NSPIRE gives your landlord 24 hours once it's documented. Get safe, take photos, report it in writing, and bring in your Housing Authority if it isn't handled. You are not asking for a favor. You're asking for the standard the law already sets.
📞
Need help right now?
For fire, a gas leak, or a medical emergency, call 911. For help finding local housing, utility, or emergency assistance, call 211, the free 24/7 helpline. For problems with your unit, contact your Public Housing Authority.

Family responsibility for household-caused defects is set out in the Housing Choice Voucher program rules (24 CFR Part 982).
