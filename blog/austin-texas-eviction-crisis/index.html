Austin was supposed to be the success story. The tech jobs came. The restaurants followed. The live music economy kept humming. And the rents — the rents went up 40%, 50%, in some neighborhoods close to 60% between 2020 and 2023, as tech workers with Silicon Valley salaries competed for apartments in a city built for a fraction of its current population.

Then the tech layoffs came. The partial rent correction came. And in 2025, Princeton University's Eviction Lab recorded something that no other city in their 48-site tracking network matched: a 30% surge in Austin eviction filings compared to baseline. Not the highest rate — cities like Atlanta, Richmond, and Charleston have higher raw rates. But the fastest acceleration. The steepest climb. The most dramatic swing in a single year.

That number tells a specific story. It is the story of what happens when a city prices out its own workforce, watches a partial correction that comes too late for the people already stretched beyond their limit, and operates under a legal framework — Texas's 3-day eviction notice, no rent control, no source-of-income protection — that gives landlords maximum speed and renters minimum margin.

Why Austin's eviction filings are surging: four factors

1
The tech boom's affordability damage that the correction didn't fix
Austin's 40–60% rent increases between 2020 and 2023 priced out a significant share of the city's service sector workforce — the restaurant workers, healthcare aides, retail employees, and caregivers who couldn't match tech salaries. Even as rents partially corrected in 2024, they remained 20–35% above pre-boom levels. Households that stretched their budgets during the peak, took on debt to cover rent gaps, or made partial payments to avoid eviction were financially depleted by the correction. The 30% eviction surge in 2025 is the financial exhaustion of that period finally breaking into filings.
2
Texas's 3-day eviction notice — the shortest in PTI's markets
Texas requires only three business days' notice before a landlord can file for eviction for non-payment — tied with Georgia for the shortest notice period in any of PTI's active markets. For a renter who missed rent because of a delayed paycheck, an unexpected medical expense, or a day care payment that hit the same week, three business days is rarely enough time to resolve a $1,200 shortfall. Texas's notice period doesn't create the affordability problem — but it converts it into a legal filing faster than any other major state.
3
State-preempted rent control and no source-of-income protection
Austin City Council attempted to pass rent stabilization measures during the peak of the housing surge. Texas state law preempted the effort — the state prohibits municipalities from enacting rent control, removing one of the tools other cities have used to moderate market-rate volatility. Texas also has no source-of-income protection requiring landlords to accept Section 8 vouchers, meaning the most financially vulnerable renters compete for housing in a fully market-rate environment with no subsidy floor.
4
Concentrated displacement of East Austin's Black and Latino communities
Austin's historically Black and Latino east-side neighborhoods absorbed the most intense gentrification pressure during the tech boom, as buyers and renters priced out of west Austin moved east, driving up rents in communities with generational roots in the area. The eviction filings tracked in Austin's east-side ZIP codes reflect not just an economic mismatch but a displacement of community — the ongoing loss of the cultural geography that defined Austin's identity before the boom.
Why a 30% surge matters more than a high raw rate Atlanta's 25% filing rate is higher than Austin's current rate. Richmond's 24% rate is higher. What makes Austin uniquely alarming is the trajectory — a 30% single-year increase means the crisis is accelerating, not stabilizing. Cities with high but stable rates have, at minimum, an established infrastructure response. A city with a surging rate is adding new families to its eviction court faster than its legal aid, emergency assistance, and housing programs can adapt. Austin's 30% surge is the leading indicator of where the crisis is going, not just where it is.

What would change this — and what you can do while waiting for it

Austin's housing advocates have argued for lengthening Texas's notice period beyond three days, implementing a right to counsel in eviction court, funding robust pre-eviction diversion, and removing the state preemption that blocked Austin's rent stabilization attempt. Some of these require state legislature action in a body that has historically sided with property owners over tenant rights.
