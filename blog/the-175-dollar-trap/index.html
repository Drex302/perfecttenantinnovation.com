The $175 Trap: Why One Eviction Filing Follows You for Years

An eviction filing doesn't end when you move. On average, the next place costs $175 more a month — and one in three renters face a new eviction threat there, too. The filing itself is the trap. Here's how to stay out of it.

By Perfect Tenant Innovation  ·  October 1, 2026  ·  For Renters  ·  Sourced from Princeton University's Eviction Lab and peer-reviewed housing research

The part no one warns you about Most people picture eviction as a single bad day — the notice, the court date, the move. But the research shows the real cost arrives afterward, and it keeps arriving for years. An eviction filing doesn't just cost you a home. It quietly raises the price of every home you rent next, and makes the next crisis more likely, not less. If you're renting on a thin margin, this is the trap to understand — and there's a way out of it.

It doesn't end when you move

Here's the sequence most renters don't see coming. You fall behind — a cut in hours, a medical bill, a car repair. A filing lands. Maybe you pay it off, maybe you even win, maybe you just move to avoid the whole thing. You think it's over. It isn't. Because the filing is now on your record, and the next landlord can see it. And what they do with it costs you for years.

Researchers who followed tenants after an eviction filing found that those who moved and kept renting paid, on average, $175 more per month at the next place — roughly a 29% increase in what they spend on rent. Think about what that means: the filing didn't just move you, it made your housing permanently more expensive, at the exact moment you had the least money to absorb it. And it doesn't stop there — one in three of those renters faced a new eviction threat at the new place, because a higher rent on the same income is a setup for the next shortfall.

The filing is the damage — not the eviction

This is the single most important thing to understand, and it's the thing the system is quietest about: the filing itself is what hurts you. Not the judgment. Not the actual removal. The filing.

In most places, an eviction filing becomes part of a record that future landlords and tenant-screening companies can see — and many of them won't rent to someone with any filing on record, even one that was dismissed, even one you paid off, even one a judge threw out. Studies have found landlords routinely refuse applicants with eviction filings regardless of how the case ended. The filing becomes a mark that follows you into every future application, and it's part of why the next place costs more and is harder to get.

Winning doesn't erase it You can be filed on in error, pay what's owed the same week, or win outright in court — and in many states the filing still stays on your record, visible to the next landlord. That's why the only real protection is keeping the filing from happening at all. Once it exists, it's hard to undo, and it starts working against you immediately.

Flipping the record from a weight into a lift

Here's the hopeful part, and it's a real one. The same system that lets rent build a negative record against you can be turned around to build a positive one for you. Every month you pay rent on time is proof that you're a reliable tenant and a reliable payer — but under the old system, that proof vanishes into your landlord's bank account and does nothing for you. It only shows up on your record when something goes wrong.

What if it worked the other way? What if the rent you're already paying built your credit, strengthened your next application, and gave you leverage the next time money got tight — instead of sitting silent until the one month you fell short?
