Keeping a Tenant Costs $1,570. Evicting One Costs $10,021.

That's not an opinion. It's the Boston Housing Authority's own math — an 84% difference between helping a struggling tenant stay and removing them. For a property owner, keeping a good tenant isn't charity. It's the better spreadsheet.

By Perfect Tenant Innovation  ·  October 1, 2026  ·  For Property Owners & Managers  ·  Sourced from eviction-diversion research and Princeton University's Eviction Lab

The Math The Real Cost of Turnover What Diversion Proves Private Diversion How PTI Does It
The question every owner should run the numbers on When a tenant falls behind, the instinct is to move toward removal — get them out, get someone new in, protect the asset. But a decade of eviction-diversion research keeps landing on the same uncomfortable finding: for the landlord, keeping the tenant is almost always cheaper than replacing them. Not kinder. Cheaper. This post is the P&L case for retention — and the system that makes it your default.

The math

The single clearest number comes from the Boston Housing Authority, which compared what it cost to maintain a struggling tenancy against what it cost to evict and replace it:

$1,570
Cost to keep a struggling tenant housed
$10,021
Cost to evict and replace that tenant

That's an 84% savings for keeping the tenant — the same unit, the same lease, kept current instead of turned over. The Syracuse Housing Authority ran its own version and saved roughly $167,500 in eviction-related costs through a diversion approach. These aren't advocacy numbers; they're operating budgets. The organizations that run both sides of the equation — the keeping and the evicting — found keeping wins by a landslide.

The real cost of turnover

Why is the gap so enormous? Because the eviction itself is the cheapest part. What follows it is where the money goes. When a tenant leaves, the costs stack up fast:

  • Lost rent during vacancy. Every week the unit sits empty is rent you'll never recover — often the single largest line.
  • Turn costs. Cleanout, repairs, paint, inspection — the physical work of making a unit rent-ready again.
  • Marketing and screening. Advertising the unit, showing it, running applications and background checks on a stranger whose payment behavior you can't yet see.
  • Court and legal fees. Filing, attorney time, and the hours of your own week the process consumes.
  • Risk on the replacement. The new tenant is an unknown. The one you removed had a track record — you just didn't have a tool to work with it.

Stack those against the cost of helping an existing, known, mostly-reliable tenant through a rough month, and the spreadsheet isn't close. A late payment is a cash-flow timing problem. A vacancy is a hole in the P&L.

What diversion proves

Eviction diversion programs — which bring landlord and tenant together before a case is decided — exist precisely because this math is real, and they've produced a track record worth paying attention to as an owner.

The outcomes In one program, nearly 90% of cases were resolved without an eviction judgment. In Durham, North Carolina, over 70% of participating families stayed in their homes — the opposite of conventional eviction court, where most families who show up receive a judgment. Philadelphia's program cut eviction filings by 41%. Across the studied programs, cases were more than twice as likely to be settled or withdrawn, and removal orders were cut in half.

Read those as a landlord and the message is simple: in roughly 9 out of 10 cases, there was a resolution available that kept the unit occupied and the rent flowing — and the only thing standing between the owner and that outcome was a structured way to reach it before the courthouse took over. Diversion programs supply that structure publicly, with mediators and sometimes rental assistance. The lesson for your own portfolio is that the structure itself is the valuable part.

Private diversion, built in

You don't have to wait for your county to run a diversion program to capture these savings. The core of what makes diversion work — early communication, a clear payment path, and a reason for both sides to choose resolution over removal — can live inside the way you manage the property itself. The goal is to make "work it out and keep the tenant" the automatic path, and "file" the rare exception, instead of the other way around.

Diversion works because it interrupts a foregone conclusion. The default in eviction court is removal. Change the default, and most tenancies survive — profitably.
