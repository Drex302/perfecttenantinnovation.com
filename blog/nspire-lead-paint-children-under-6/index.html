Children Under 6 Trigger Enhanced Lead Paint Standards Under NSPIRE.
The Question the Inspector Asks First.

Before an NSPIRE inspection of a Section 8 unit begins, the inspector asks one question: does a child under 6 live here, or are they expected to? In pre-1978 housing, the answer changes how deteriorated paint is scored — turning a lower-tier issue into a Severe defect and requiring lead-safe work practices for every correction.

By Perfect Tenant Innovation  ·  September 2026  ·  9 min read

Source: HUD NSPIRE HCV/PBV Inspection Checklist (OMB 2577-0289) · HUD Lead Safe Housing Rule (24 CFR Part 35) · EPA RRP Rule · CDC childhood lead data

The opening question Pre-1978 housing Why children under 6 How paint is scored Lead-safe work practices Action steps

Every NSPIRE inspection of a Housing Choice Voucher unit begins with a question that determines how a significant portion of the inspection will be scored — before the inspector looks at a single wall. The question is whether a child under the age of 6 resides in the unit, or is expected to reside there. For Section 8 landlords with pre-1978 housing, the answer to that question can be the difference between a deteriorated-paint finding that's a minor issue and one that's a Severe defect requiring specialized, lead-safe correction.

This isn't an arbitrary bureaucratic distinction. Childhood lead exposure is one of the most well-documented and most preventable environmental health hazards in American housing. Lead-based paint — banned for residential use in 1978 but still present in millions of older homes — is the primary source of childhood lead poisoning, and children under 6 are the most vulnerable because of how their developing bodies absorb and are affected by lead. NSPIRE's enhanced standards for units housing young children reflect that reality.

This post explains exactly how the rule works: the opening question, the pre-1978 housing trigger, why children under 6 specifically, how deteriorated paint is scored differently when a young child is present, and what lead-safe work practices a landlord must follow to correct a finding.

The question the inspector asks before the inspection begins

🧒 Asked at the start of every HCV inspection
"Do any children under the age of 6 reside in the unit, or are they expected to reside in the unit?"
If YES (and housing is pre-1978)
Lead-based paint visual assessment requirements are triggered. Deteriorated paint above the de minimis threshold is scored as a Severe defect. Any correction must follow lead-safe work practices. The child's presence elevates the consequences of every paint-related finding.
If NO (or housing is 1978+)
Standard paint condition assessment applies. Deteriorated paint is still evaluated, but the enhanced lead-based paint provisions specific to protecting young children are not triggered in the same way. The heightened scoring does not apply.

The phrase "or are they expected to reside" matters. This isn't only about children currently living in the unit — it includes a child expected to move in, such as an expectant parent's coming baby or a family relocating with young children. A landlord cannot avoid the enhanced standards by timing an inspection for a window when a child happens to be staying elsewhere. The standard follows the reasonable expectation of a young child's presence in the unit.

The pre-1978 housing trigger

The enhanced lead-based paint requirements apply specifically to "target housing" — residential housing constructed before 1978. This date is the pivot point of the entire lead-based paint regulatory framework, because 1978 is the year the federal government banned lead-based paint for residential use. Housing built after that date is presumed not to contain lead-based paint and is generally exempt from the assessment requirements.

Pre-1978
Target housing — lead paint rules apply
Housing built before 1978 is presumed to potentially contain lead-based paint. With a child under 6 present, the full enhanced NSPIRE lead paint provisions are triggered — visual assessment, Severe scoring for deteriorated paint, and lead-safe correction requirements.
1978+
Generally exempt from lead paint assessment
Housing built in 1978 or later is presumed not to contain lead-based paint, since the residential ban took effect that year. The enhanced lead-based paint assessment requirements generally do not apply, though deteriorated paint is still evaluated under standard condition provisions.
Know the construction year of every unit in your portfolio The single most important piece of information for lead paint compliance is the year each unit was built. If you own pre-1978 housing and rent to families, you are in the population of landlords for whom this rule is most consequential. Verify the construction date through your property records, county assessor data, or the deed. If a unit was built before 1978 and a family with a young child lives there, the enhanced NSPIRE lead paint provisions apply to every inspection — and you should be managing to them year-round.

Why children under 6 specifically

🧒 The health basis for the standard
Children under 6 absorb lead more readily than adults — and the damage to a developing brain is irreversible.
Young children are uniquely vulnerable to lead exposure for several reasons. Their bodies absorb a far higher percentage of ingested lead than adults do. Their developing brains and nervous systems are acutely sensitive to lead's neurotoxic effects. And their normal behavior — crawling on floors, putting hands and objects in their mouths, teething on windowsills and other surfaces — puts them in direct, repeated contact with the lead paint dust and chips that deteriorated paint produces. The CDC has established that there is no safe blood lead level in children. Even low levels of lead exposure in early childhood are associated with reduced IQ, attention and behavioral problems, and developmental delays that do not reverse. NSPIRE's enhanced standard for units with children under 6 exists because this is the population and the age window where prevention matters most and the consequences of failure are permanent.

This is why NSPIRE treats a deteriorated-paint finding in a pre-1978 unit with a young child so much more seriously than the same finding in a unit without one. The physical condition — chipping, peeling, flaking paint — is identical in both cases. What differs is the risk. A flake of lead paint on the floor of a unit with no young children is a maintenance issue. The same flake on the floor of a unit where a 2-year-old crawls and mouths objects is a direct pathway to irreversible harm. NSPIRE scores the risk, and the risk is defined by the presence of the child.

How deteriorated paint is scored differently when a child under 6 is present

Deteriorated paint — no child under 6
Standard scoring
Deteriorated paint is evaluated under standard condition provisions. Depending on extent and location, it may be scored as a lower-tier defect. The paint condition is assessed for its physical impact, not for lead-exposure risk to a young child.
Deteriorated paint — child under 6 in pre-1978 unit
Severe defect
Deteriorated paint above the de minimis threshold is scored as a Severe defect — 30-day correction required. And the correction itself must follow lead-safe work practices. The child's presence elevates both the severity and the correction method.

The concept of the "de minimis threshold" is important here. Federal lead-safe housing rules recognize that a very small amount of deteriorated paint — below a defined minimum surface area — does not trigger the full lead-safe work practice requirements. But above that threshold, the deteriorated paint in a pre-1978 unit with a young child is a Severe NSPIRE defect. The de minimis levels under the HUD Lead Safe Housing Rule are generally defined as small areas: for example, more than 20 square feet on exterior surfaces, more than 2 square feet in any one interior room or space, or more than 10 percent of the total surface area of a small component like a windowsill or trim. Deterioration above these thresholds requires lead-safe correction.

What NSPIRE inspectors look for in the visual assessment

🪟 Windows and sills
Window sills and troughs are the highest-risk location — friction from opening and closing windows generates lead dust, and sills are at a child's mouth height. Deteriorated paint here is scrutinized closely.
🚪 Doors and door frames
Friction and impact surfaces on doors and frames generate lead paint chips and dust. Peeling or chipping paint on these surfaces is a common finding in older housing.
🧱 Walls and ceilings
Large surface areas where peeling, flaking, or chipping paint exceeds the de minimis threshold. Water-damaged areas where paint has bubbled or detached are examined.
🪜 Trim, baseboards, railings
Small components — trim, baseboards, stair railings, banisters — where the de minimis threshold is a percentage of the component's surface area rather than a fixed square footage.
🏠 Porches and exterior
Exterior deteriorated paint on porches, railings, and siding where children play. The exterior de minimis threshold is larger but still triggers requirements when exceeded.
🔨 Friction and impact surfaces
Any surface subject to friction (windows, drawers) or impact (door strikes, baseboards) — these generate the lead dust that is the primary childhood exposure pathway.

Lead-safe work practices — how corrections must be done

When deteriorated paint in a pre-1978 unit with a child under 6 must be corrected, the correction cannot be done with ordinary painting practices. Scraping, sanding, or repainting deteriorated lead-based paint using standard methods generates lead dust — the exact hazard the rule exists to prevent. Corrections must follow lead-safe work practices under the HUD Lead Safe Housing Rule and the EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) Rule.

👷
Use EPA RRP-certified renovators for the work
Work that disturbs painted surfaces in pre-1978 housing where children under 6 reside must generally be performed by, or under the supervision of, an EPA-certified Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) certified renovator, or firm. Verify certification before engaging a contractor for lead-related work.
🚧
Contain the work area to prevent dust spread
Lead-safe work practices require containing the work area with plastic sheeting, restricting access, and preventing the spread of lead dust to other parts of the unit. The containment protects the child and other occupants during the correction itself.
🚫
Prohibited methods — no open-flame, no power sanding without HEPA
Certain methods are prohibited because they aerosolize lead: open-flame burning or torching, machine sanding or grinding without HEPA exhaust control, and uncontained dry scraping of large areas. These methods create severe lead dust hazards and are not permitted for lead-safe correction.
🧹
Specialized cleanup and clearance
After the work, specialized cleanup using HEPA vacuuming and wet-wiping is required to remove lead dust. Depending on the scope, clearance examination or dust testing may be required to confirm the area is safe for the child's return before the correction is considered complete.
Correcting lead paint the wrong way is worse than not correcting it A landlord who tries to correct deteriorated lead paint quickly by dry-scraping or power-sanding it — to pass an NSPIRE inspection — can generate a massive lead dust hazard that poisons the very child the rule protects. The correction method is not optional. If deteriorated paint in a unit with a young child needs correction, engage an EPA RRP-certified professional and follow lead-safe practices. Doing the work improperly exposes the child to acute harm and exposes you to significant liability under federal lead-safety law that goes well beyond an NSPIRE deficiency.

Action steps: lead paint compliance for pre-1978 units with young children

1
Confirm the construction year of every unit and flag all pre-1978 properties
Pull the construction date for every unit in your portfolio from county assessor records or your deeds. Flag every pre-1978 unit. These are the properties where the enhanced lead paint standard applies when a young child is present — and you need to know which ones they are before an inspector asks the opening question.
2
Know which of your pre-1978 units currently house — or may soon house — a child under 6
For each pre-1978 unit, know whether a family with a young child lives there, or one is expected. These are the highest-priority units for proactive paint maintenance. A pre-1978 unit with a toddler is a unit where every deteriorated-paint finding is a Severe defect requiring specialized correction — so prevention is far cheaper than reaction.
3
Inspect painted surfaces proactively — before deterioration exceeds the threshold
In pre-1978 units with young children, inspect windows, sills, doors, trim, and high-friction surfaces regularly. Address paint deterioration while it's still small — below the de minimis threshold — using lead-safe practices. Catching a chipping windowsill early and correcting it properly prevents both the Severe NSPIRE finding and the child's exposure.
4
Build a relationship with an EPA RRP-certified contractor before you need one
When a deteriorated-paint correction is required in a unit with a young child, you cannot use just any painter — you need lead-safe certified work. Identify and establish a relationship with an EPA RRP-certified renovator or firm in your market now, so that when a 30-day correction clock is running, you have a qualified professional ready rather than scrambling to find one.
5
Document all lead-related disclosures, assessments, and corrections
Federal law requires lead-based paint disclosure to tenants in pre-1978 housing, and NSPIRE compliance requires documentation of corrections. Keep records of the lead disclosure provided to each tenant, any lead assessments performed, and the scope and certification of any lead-safe correction work. This documentation protects the child, satisfies the PHA, and protects you legally.
This is the NSPIRE standard where doing right by the tenant and passing the inspection are the same thing Every NSPIRE standard is ultimately about tenant safety, but the lead paint rule for children under 6 makes the connection unmistakable. A landlord who maintains sound paint surfaces in a pre-1978 unit with a toddler isn't just passing an inspection — they're protecting a child from irreversible neurological harm during the most vulnerable window of that child's development. Meet this standard proactively, correct deteriorated paint the right way, and both the inspection and the child are protected. There is no version of this rule where cutting the corner is the right move.
