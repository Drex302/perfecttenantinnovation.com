Greenville, South Carolina: The Eviction Crisis Behind the "Overnight Success Story"

The headline everyone's reading Three weeks ago, CNN Travel named Greenville, South Carolina the No. 3 best town to visit in America for 2026, praising its "50-year overnight success story" — a hollowed-out textile town turned into one of the most admired downtown revivals in the country. Forbes called it one of America's ten best revitalized downtowns back in 2011. The Rudy Bruner Award for Urban Excellence gave it a national prize in 2015. This post is about the other headline — the one that runs underneath.

A 50-year overnight success story

Downtown Greenville's turnaround is real, and it's been earned the hard way. Through the 1960s and '70s, the city's textile mills — once so dominant that Greenville called itself "the Textile Center of the World" — shut down or moved overseas, and Main Street hollowed out along with them. What followed was four decades of deliberate, patient civic work: a 1968 downtown development plan, a $34 million public-private anchor project in 1982, the removal of a four-lane bridge over the Reedy River in 2002 to build Falls Park and the Liberty Bridge, and a steady accumulation of national attention that culminated this year in CNN calling it a "50-year overnight success story."

That story is genuinely worth telling. It's also, on its own, an incomplete map of the city. A downtown built around walkability, a waterfall, and a Michelin-starred restaurant sits inside a metro area where — over the same 12-month window Eviction Lab was measuring America's eviction crisis — Greenville posted the second-highest eviction filing rate of any city the Lab tracks in the country.

Only Richmond, Virginia — at 24% — ranked worse. Charleston came in third at 16%. Two of South Carolina's most nationally celebrated cities sit inside the top three eviction rates in the entire country, in the very same period both were drawing magazine spreads and "best places to visit" lists.

Zoom in further and the pattern holds. According to the Princeton Eviction Lab, Greenville County's eviction filing rate has run around 25% — more than triple the national average of 8%, and above even South Carolina's own elevated state average of 23%. Statewide, the ACLU of South Carolina found that Black renters represent nearly a third of all eviction filings despite making up roughly a fifth of adult renters — and that more than 99% of South Carolinians facing eviction have no legal representation in court.

Why this matters for the "success story" framing Eviction data and downtown revitalization data don't usually appear in the same article. That's not an accident of journalism — it's a structural fact about how "revitalization" gets measured. Foot traffic, restaurant openings, and Forbes rankings are counted downtown. Eviction filings are counted in housing court, a few miles away, in neighborhoods that rarely make the postcard.

The neighborhoods the renaissance is emptying

Greenville's downtown didn't spring up in a vacuum. It's ringed by a set of historic neighborhoods — Nicholtown, West Greenville, Southernside, Haynie-Sirrine, Sans Souci — that were majority-Black for generations, in large part because decades-old discriminatory real estate practices concentrated Black residents there in the first place. Now that downtown's comeback has made proximity to it valuable, those same neighborhoods are the ones absorbing the cost.

Nicholtown

Black population fell from 4,540 in 1990 to 2,634 in 2020, while the white population more than doubled over the same period, per Furman University research.

West Greenville

Once considered undesirable real estate; properties now routinely sell for $300,000–$500,000, far above what's affordable at area median income.

Southernside

One of the four Special Emphasis Neighborhoods tracked by city planners showing the sharpest recent decline in Black population share.

Haynie-Sirrine & Sans Souci

Named alongside Nicholtown and Southernside as neighborhoods where Black households are being priced out as white residents move back toward downtown.

A home on Underwood Avenue in Nicholtown that would have sold for a fraction of today's price a decade ago is now valued at nearly four times that amount. The pattern is what researchers call "reverse flight" — the same affluent households that abandoned these neighborhoods for the suburbs a generation ago are now moving back in, drawn by exactly the downtown proximity the city spent thirty years building. The families who stayed through the decades when no one wanted to live there are the ones now priced out of staying for the decades everyone does.

The Renaissance Tax

There's a number that explains why this keeps happening, and it isn't an eviction statistic. According to the research team at Furman University's Center of Applied Sustainability Research, the median white household income in Greenville is approximately $74,000 a year. The median Black household income is approximately $28,000 — a gap of roughly $46,000, or nearly three-to-one.

Call it the Renaissance Tax: the price historically Black Greenville pays, in displacement and eviction exposure, for a comeback that historically white Greenville is positioned to benefit from. It isn't a coincidence that eviction filing rates and gentrification pressure are both concentrated in the same handful of neighborhoods. Rising property values that make a house a windfall for one owner make it an eviction risk for the renter down the block — especially when rents rise faster than a household earning a third of the citywide median can absorb, and South Carolina places no cap on how much a landlord can raise rent.

"It means that white people with money can move where they want and Black people living in poverty must scramble." — housing researcher quoted on displacement patterns in Greenville's historically Black neighborhoods

A city trying to slow its own success

To Greenville's credit, this isn't a story with no resistance in it. In January 2024, after years of community concern, Greenville City Council voted to buy a 2.1-acre parcel in Nicholtown near First Baptist Greenville for $350,000 — specifically to stop a developer from building homes residents feared would accelerate displacement. It was only the second time the city had used its open-space acquisition fund this way. The Nicholtown Neighborhood Association called it a real, if partial, win.

But a single land purchase is not a housing policy, and residents at recent development meetings in Nicholtown have continued to describe developers showing up with "the bare minimum of information" and no traffic studies or renderings — the same pattern of being outpaced by capital that has defined the last three decades. The tools that exist to slow displacement in Greenville are real. They are also, so far, smaller than the forces working against them.

Where PTI fits

Perfect Tenant Innovation isn't a substitute for a land trust, a zoning reform, or a tenant union — Greenville has all three fighting for its historically Black neighborhoods right now, and they matter more than any piece of software. What PTI can do is remove one of the specific pressures that turns a renter with a temporary shortfall into an eviction filing statistic: a system that rewards on-time payment, builds credit instead of just extracting rent, and gives landlords a reason to work with a tenant through a rough month instead of filing.

Greenville doesn't need to be rescued from its own renaissance. It needs the renaissance to actually include the neighborhoods that were here before it started.
