Charleston, South Carolina draws more than seven million visitors a year. Its historic district is one of the most photographed in the country. Its restaurant scene has won national awards. Its waterfront real estate is among the most coveted on the East Coast. And in 2025, one in every six of its renters received an eviction filing.

That number — a 17% eviction filing rate, third-highest of any city tracked by Princeton University's Eviction Lab — sits alongside the tourism brochures and the Food & Wine covers without contradiction in the way that only truly unequal cities can manage. Two economies occupy the same geography. One thrives. One files notices.

And South Carolina as a state has made the Eviction Lab's watchlist in a way no other state has: two of its cities — Charleston at 17% and Greenville at 20% — occupy the third and second spots in the national rankings. The state does not have a housing stability problem. It has a housing stability crisis, distributed across its major population centers.

Black renters in South Carolina are disproportionately represented in eviction filings statewide — a pattern consistent with Eviction Lab's national finding that Black renters make up 28% of the tracked renter population but 39% of eviction defendants. Charleston's historically Black communities — North Charleston, the West Ashley corridor, and neighborhoods along the former Neck Area — carry concentrated eviction pressure as gentrification pushes westward from the peninsula.

Why Charleston's eviction rate is this high: four compounding forces

1
Tourism-driven rent inflation in a low-wage service economy
Charleston's tourism economy generates billions in annual revenue — but the workers who staff its hotels, restaurants, and historic attractions earn service-sector wages while competing for housing in a market priced for short-term rental income and second-home buyers. A hospitality worker earning $15–$18 per hour cannot afford market-rate rent in a city where median two-bedroom rents have climbed past $1,600. The math of that mismatch produces eviction filings at scale.
2
Short-term rental conversion removing long-term housing supply
Charleston's Airbnb and short-term rental market has converted thousands of units from long-term residential housing to visitor accommodation. Each unit removed from the long-term rental market tightens supply and pushes prices higher for the renters who remain. Charleston has attempted STR regulation but enforcement has been inconsistent, and the conversion pipeline continues to drain affordable units from the market.
3
South Carolina's 5-day pay-or-quit notice and landlord-favorable statutes
South Carolina requires only five days' notice before a landlord can file for eviction for non-payment. The state's landlord-tenant law offers limited tenant protections compared to peer states — no rent control, no source-of-income protection, no guaranteed right to counsel in eviction proceedings. When legal infrastructure consistently favors the filing party, filing rates rise. Charleston's rate reflects that legal environment as much as its economic one.
4
Climate displacement pressure compounding housing scarcity
Charleston is one of the most flood-prone cities on the East Coast, and climate-driven flooding events have displaced residents from historically affordable neighborhoods, particularly in North Charleston and lower-elevation areas. Displaced renters competing for a shrinking affordable housing stock in a tourism-priced market face compounding pressure. Climate risk and housing instability are not separate crises in Charleston — they are the same crisis from two angles.
South Carolina's statewide pattern Charleston is not an outlier within South Carolina — it is the visible tip of a statewide crisis. Greenville's 20% rate was second in the nation before recent data updates. Both cities share the same structural conditions: low-wage service economies, limited tenant protections, and a housing market that has outpaced the incomes of its workforce. When two cities from the same state occupy the top three nationally, the problem is not local. It is policy.

What would actually fix this — and what you can do while it doesn't

Housing advocates in South Carolina have identified the policy changes that would reduce Charleston's eviction rate: lengthening the notice period beyond five days, implementing source-of-income protections that prevent landlords from screening out voucher holders, providing right to counsel in eviction proceedings, and regulating short-term rental conversion to protect long-term housing supply. Some of these changes require state legislation — which means sustained political pressure on a legislature that has historically favored property owners over tenants.
