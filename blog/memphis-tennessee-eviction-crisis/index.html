Memphis tenants go to eviction court and lose 98% of the time.

That number — from a Legal Services Corporation analysis of Shelby County General Sessions Civil Court records — is the most concise summary of what Memphis renters face when a notice arrives. Not a system that weighs the case. Not a proceeding where the landlord's own obligations are examined. A process in which the tenant prevails in fewer than 2 out of every 100 cases filed against them.

In 2025, 27,658 eviction cases were filed in Shelby County. Memphis is a majority-Black city where 67% of renters are Black. The Princeton University Eviction Lab estimates that 81% of the renters who faced eviction between April 2024 and April 2025 were Black. That 14-percentage-point gap — 67% of renters, 81% of defendants — is the racial disparity of Memphis's eviction crisis rendered in the clearest possible numbers.

And the entity filing those cases? In more than two-thirds of the top 100 evicting properties, it isn't a local landlord. It's a company with a mailing address in New York, Florida, Pennsylvania, Utah, New Jersey, or Nevada — owning apartments in North, South, and East Memphis neighborhoods while the eviction filings flow from a thousand miles away.

"Around 81% of the renters who faced eviction in Memphis between April 1, 2024, and April 1, 2025, were Black — despite only 67% of all Memphis renters being Black."

Those 100 properties together filed nearly 10,000 eviction cases — approximately one-third of all Shelby County filings in 2025. The top evicting complexes are clustered in North, South, and East Memphis: neighborhoods including Whitehaven, Berclair, and Hickory Hill. These are the same neighborhoods where the concentration of Black renters is highest and the eviction filing rates run above the city average.

The absentee landlord model — how it works against Memphis renters When the owner of a Memphis apartment complex has a New York or Nevada mailing address, the property management decisions — when to file, when to negotiate, when to offer a payment plan — are made by people who have never seen the neighborhood, never met the tenant, and are optimizing for asset performance from a distance. Research consistently shows that large, out-of-state corporate landlords have higher eviction filing rates than local individual landlords. Memphis's concentration of out-of-state ownership in its highest-filing neighborhoods is not coincidental. It is the mechanism.

What the research says about what eviction does to Memphis renters

Memphis is one of a small number of American cities where researchers have documented the direct relationship between eviction filing rates and health outcomes at the neighborhood level. A peer-reviewed study combining Shelby County eviction court records with public health data found what advocates had long observed clinically: eviction doesn't just take housing. It takes health.

🧠
Mental health outcomes
Higher eviction filing rates in Memphis neighborhoods were significantly associated with poorer self-rated mental health — even after controlling for income, poverty, and other neighborhood characteristics. The threat of eviction alone, not just the displacement itself, produces measurable mental health damage.
🏥
Compounding health disparities
Memphis already carries disproportionate rates of breast cancer outcomes, infant mortality, pediatric asthma, and chronic disease in its Black communities. Researchers identified housing instability — and eviction specifically — as a fundamental cause of these health inequities, not a separate problem alongside them.
👶
Children in the data
Parents in Memphis are more likely to face eviction than non-parents — a national pattern documented by Eviction Lab that concentrates housing instability precisely where children are present. School disruption, healthcare access loss, and developmental stress follow the eviction filing into the household.
📊
Market characteristics don't explain it
University of Memphis researchers found that market characteristics alone — rent levels, property values, poverty rates — are insufficient to explain the clustering of evictions in Black neighborhoods. The racial composition of the neighborhood is a predictor of eviction rates independent of economic factors.

Why Memphis's eviction rate stays this high: four structural factors

1
Tennessee's 14-day notice — and a court system where tenants win 2% of cases
Tennessee requires landlords to give 14 days' notice before filing for eviction for non-payment — longer than Georgia's 3 days or Virginia's 5 days, but the court process that follows is arguably the most tenant-unfavorable in PTI's markets. Tenants prevailed in fewer than 2% of Shelby County eviction cases analyzed by the Legal Services Corporation. Without legal representation, most tenants default. Without default protection, the judgment follows them indefinitely.
2
Out-of-state corporate ownership concentrated in majority-Black neighborhoods
Two-thirds of the top 100 evicting properties in Memphis are owned by out-of-state entities. Corporate landlords optimizing rental portfolios from New York and Nevada make eviction filing decisions as financial management rather than human relationship. The neighborhoods most affected — Whitehaven, Berclair, Hickory Hill — are majority-Black communities where the concentration of absentee corporate ownership is highest.
3
Eviction filing rates averaging 30,000+ annually for nearly a decade
Eviction filings in Shelby County averaged more than 30,000 annually between 2016 and 2019 — before the pandemic, before the moratoriums, before any of the conditions that other cities cite as explanations for their recent surges. Memphis's eviction crisis is not a pandemic-era aberration. It is a structural condition with a decade-long baseline. The COVID moratoriums temporarily suppressed it. The underlying system that produces it never changed.
4
Fastest-growing rental market with wages that haven't kept pace
Researchers have identified Memphis as the fastest-growing rental market in the United States at its peak — a designation that reflects demand outpacing affordable supply, not prosperity reaching existing residents. When a rental market grows fastest in the neighborhoods with the highest poverty rates and the lowest wage growth, the math produces eviction filings. Memphis's growth has not been shared evenly, and its eviction rates reflect that asymmetry directly.

What would actually change this — and what you can do now

Memphis housing advocates and researchers have identified the interventions that would reduce Shelby County's eviction rate: universal access to legal representation in eviction court (which has been shown to dramatically reduce default judgments), regulation of out-of-state corporate landlord eviction practices, tenant notification of the Shelby County Eviction Settlement Program before cases reach court, and longer notice periods that give renters a realistic window to resolve payment issues.

Changing a system where tenants win 2% of cases requires political will, sustained advocacy, and time. Memphis renters do not have the luxury of waiting for that change to come before they need a stable place to live.
