Unvented Heaters Are an Automatic NSPIRE Life-Threatening Fail.
Even in a Unit That's 75 Degrees.

An unvented space heater that burns gas, oil, or kerosene is an automatic Life-Threatening defect under NSPIRE — 24-hour correction required. It doesn't matter who brought it in, how long it's been there, or how warm the unit is. If it's present at inspection, your unit fails.

Presence alone is the fail — the heater does not need to be running
NSPIRE classifies the mere presence of an unvented gas, oil, or kerosene space heater in a unit as a Life-Threatening defect. The inspector does not need to see it in use. An unplugged, unlit, or empty unvented fuel-burning heater sitting in a corner is a 24-hour Life-Threatening fail.

Of all the Life-Threatening deficiencies in the NSPIRE framework, the unvented heater is the one landlords most often fail on through no direct action of their own — because it's the one a tenant is most likely to introduce into the unit without the landlord's knowledge. A tenant whose heat isn't keeping up buys a kerosene heater at the hardware store. A tenant trying to save on the gas bill uses an unvented propane heater in the living room. A tenant with a drafty bedroom brings in an old oil-burning space heater from a relative. In each case, the tenant is trying to stay warm — and in each case, they've created a 24-hour Life-Threatening NSPIRE fail on the landlord's HCV contract.

This post explains exactly what NSPIRE prohibits, why it's classified at the highest severity tier, why it doesn't matter who introduced the heater, and — most importantly — what landlords must do to manage this risk that originates outside their direct control.

The rule, exactly as NSPIRE states it

The NSPIRE HCV checklist classifies as Life-Threatening: an unvented space heater that burns gas, oil, or kerosene present in the unit. The correction timeframe is 24 hours — the same as a missing smoke alarm, a missing carbon monoxide alarm, or an exposed electrical conductor. It sits in the highest severity tier NSPIRE has.

The key words are unvented and burns gas, oil, or kerosene. A heater is "unvented" when it releases its combustion byproducts directly into the room rather than channeling them to the exterior through a flue, chimney, or vent pipe. When a device burns a carbon-based fuel — natural gas, propane, heating oil, or kerosene — and has no vent, every byproduct of that combustion, including carbon monoxide, water vapor, and nitrogen dioxide, goes directly into the air the tenant breathes.

What's banned and what's allowed

✗ Automatic Life-Threatening fail
  • Unvented kerosene space heaters
  • Unvented natural gas space heaters
  • Unvented propane (LP) space heaters
  • Unvented oil-burning space heaters
  • "Ventless" gas fireplaces or logs without proper venting
  • Portable propane "buddy" heaters intended for outdoor/garage use
  • Any fuel-burning heater with no flue, chimney, or exterior vent
✓ Permitted heating sources
  • Central forced-air HVAC (gas or electric, properly vented)
  • Electric baseboard heaters (no combustion)
  • Electric space heaters (no combustion — but not a permanent source)
  • Radiator systems on a building boiler
  • Heat pumps (no combustion)
  • Properly vented gas furnaces with intact exhaust
  • Electric wall heaters permanently installed
The distinction is combustion + venting — not portability A portable electric space heater is not an NSPIRE Life-Threatening fail on the unvented-heater rule — it doesn't burn fuel, so it produces no combustion byproducts. (It still doesn't count as a permanently installed heating source for the 64°F heating requirement — see our heating post — but it's not the Life-Threatening violation this post covers.) The Life-Threatening rule is specifically about combustion heaters with no venting. Electric heaters combust nothing. Gas, oil, and kerosene heaters combust fuel — and if they don't vent it, they fail.

Why NSPIRE classifies this at the highest severity tier

☠️
Carbon monoxide poisoning
Incomplete combustion in any fuel-burning heater produces carbon monoxide. An unvented heater releases it directly into the sealed living space. In a closed room overnight, CO concentrations can reach fatal levels while occupants sleep. This is the primary reason for the Life-Threatening classification.
🔥
Fire risk
Open-flame and hot-surface fuel-burning heaters ignite nearby combustibles — curtains, bedding, furniture, clothing. Kerosene and propane heaters are a leading cause of residential heating fires, particularly when knocked over or placed too close to flammable materials.
💨
Oxygen depletion & NO₂
Combustion consumes oxygen and releases nitrogen dioxide and water vapor into the room. In a tightly sealed unit, oxygen depletion and NO₂ accumulation cause headaches, respiratory distress, and — combined with CO — a compounding health hazard, especially for children and the elderly.
💧
Moisture & secondary mold
Combustion produces water vapor. An unvented heater running for weeks pumps moisture into the unit — which, as covered in our mold post, is itself a Moderate NSPIRE defect and a precursor to the mold conditions that produce Severe and Life-Threatening findings.

The compounding nature of these risks is why NSPIRE doesn't grade unvented heaters on a sliding scale the way it grades mold or temperature. There is no "moderate" unvented heater. The presence of the device is the hazard, and the hazard is immediate — so the classification is Life-Threatening and the correction window is 24 hours.

Why it doesn't matter who brought the heater in

🔥 The scenario that catches good landlords
The inspector arrives in January. The unit is a comfortable 74°F. In the bedroom corner sits a kerosene heater the tenant bought last week.
The landlord's furnace is working perfectly. The temperature passes. The landlord never knew the tenant bought a kerosene heater — the tenant just wanted extra warmth in a cold bedroom and picked one up at the hardware store. It doesn't matter. NSPIRE scores the condition of the unit at inspection, not the landlord's intent or knowledge. The unvented kerosene heater is present. That's an automatic Life-Threatening fail on the landlord's HCV contract, with a 24-hour correction clock.
→ Life-Threatening · 24-hour correction · Attributed to the landlord's contract regardless of who brought the heater in

This is the hard truth of the unvented heater rule: NSPIRE evaluates the physical condition of the unit, not the chain of responsibility for how it got that way. A tenant-introduced hazard is still a hazard, and it's still the landlord's HCV contract that absorbs the fail. This is fundamentally different from most other NSPIRE deficiencies, which arise from the building's condition and maintenance — things squarely within the landlord's control. The unvented heater is the deficiency most likely to walk into the unit in a shopping bag.

That reality shapes the entire landlord response. You cannot prevent a tenant from buying a kerosene heater the way you can prevent a leaky roof by maintaining the roof. What you can do is: communicate clearly and in writing that unvented fuel-burning heaters are prohibited, ensure the permanent heating system works well enough that tenants don't feel they need a supplemental heater, and walk every unit before every inspection to catch and remove any that have appeared.

An unvented heater used to compensate for a broken furnace creates two Life-Threatening fails If a tenant introduces an unvented heater because the permanent heating system isn't working, the landlord now faces two separate Life-Threatening deficiencies: the non-functioning heating source during heating season, and the unvented heater itself. One underlying problem — a furnace that isn't keeping up — produces two 24-hour correction clocks. This is why keeping the permanent heating system in good working order is itself the best defense against the unvented heater fail.

Action steps: managing a risk that originates outside your control

1
Keep the permanent heating system working well — the best prevention is a warm unit
Tenants introduce supplemental heaters when they're cold. A permanent heating system that reliably maintains 68°F or above throughout the unit removes the motivation to buy a kerosene or propane heater in the first place. Your September HVAC service — covered in our heating post — is also your primary defense against the unvented heater fail. A warm tenant doesn't shop for space heaters.
2
Put a written no-unvented-heater policy in every lease and every heating-season reminder
State clearly and in writing — in the lease and in an annual pre-heating-season notice — that unvented gas, oil, and kerosene heaters are prohibited in the unit, that their presence violates federal housing inspection standards, and that they create a serious carbon monoxide and fire risk. A tenant who understands the rule and the reason is far less likely to introduce the hazard.
3
Walk every unit before every NSPIRE inspection — specifically looking for supplemental heaters
During your pre-inspection walkthrough, look specifically for any fuel-burning space heater. Check bedrooms, living areas, basements, and enclosed porches. If you find an unvented kerosene, propane, or oil heater, remove it before the inspection — and talk to the tenant about why the permanent heating system needs attention if they felt they needed it.
4
Respond immediately to any tenant report that "the heat isn't enough"
A tenant complaint about inadequate heat is an early warning that they may be about to introduce a supplemental heater — or already have. Treat every "my unit is cold" report as urgent. Diagnose and fix the permanent heating deficiency quickly, and confirm the tenant hasn't brought in an unvented heater as a stopgap. A fast response to the cold complaint prevents the Life-Threatening fail.
5
If a tenant needs supplemental heat, provide a permitted option
If a unit genuinely has a cold room that the permanent system struggles to reach, provide the tenant with a permitted electric space heater as an interim measure while you address the underlying heating deficiency. An electric heater produces no combustion byproducts and is not the Life-Threatening violation. Giving the tenant a safe option removes the incentive for them to buy an unsafe one.

A tenant notice template you can use

Send this — or something like it — to every HCV tenant before heating season begins each year. A clear, friendly, reasoned notice is your best tool for preventing a tenant-introduced Life-Threatening fail.

📋 Sample pre-heating-season tenant notice
Important: Heating Safety Notice

As the heating season begins, we want to make sure your home stays warm and safe. Please note an important safety rule that also protects your housing:

Unvented gas, propane, oil, and kerosene space heaters are not permitted in your unit. These heaters release carbon monoxide — an invisible, odorless gas that can be deadly — directly into your living space. They are also a federal housing inspection violation that can put your housing assistance at risk.

If your unit is not staying warm enough, please contact us right away. Do not buy a kerosene or propane heater. Instead, call us — it is our responsibility to make sure your heating system keeps your home at a safe, comfortable temperature, and we will address any heating problem quickly. If needed, we can provide a safe electric heater while we make repairs.

Your safety is our priority. Please reach out at the first sign of any heating problem.
The unvented heater fail is the most preventable Life-Threatening deficiency — through communication, not construction Most Life-Threatening NSPIRE deficiencies are prevented through maintenance — fixing wiring, servicing furnaces, installing alarms. The unvented heater is prevented through communication and responsiveness: a warm unit, a clear written policy, a fast response to cold complaints, and a pre-inspection walkthrough. It costs almost nothing and requires no contractor. The landlords who fail on this item are the ones who never told their tenants the rule and never walked the unit before the inspector did.
