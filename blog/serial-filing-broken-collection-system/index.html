Serial Eviction Filing Is a Symptom of a Broken Collection System

If you're filing eviction to get paid — not to remove a tenant — the courthouse has quietly become your billing department. It's the most expensive one you could possibly use.

By Perfect Tenant Innovation  ·  October 1, 2026  ·  For Property Owners & Managers  ·  Sourced from Princeton University's Eviction Lab

Let's be honest about what filing often is Eviction is sold as a last resort — the thing you do when a tenant has to go. But Princeton's Eviction Lab, examining millions of cases, found something most owners already quietly know: a large share of filings aren't meant to remove anyone. They're meant to collect. The same tenant, the same unit, filed on month after month. Researchers call it serial filing, and if you've ever filed on a tenant you fully intended to keep, this post is written for you — not to scold you, but because there's a cheaper way to get the same money.

The pattern in the data

When the Eviction Lab assembled its national database, researchers noticed something they didn't expect: the same landlords filing against the same people at the same addresses, over and over, across months and sometimes years. Published in Social Forces, their analysis named the behavior serial eviction filing — and found it was neither rare nor random. It clusters, predictably, in two places: among larger and corporate operators, and in jurisdictions where filing is cheap and fast.

In Washington, D.C., 64% of eviction filings belonged to a series of at least two filings against the same household within six months — yet most never ended in an actual removal. In interviews, landlords and property managers in Baltimore, Dallas, and Cleveland told researchers plainly that they rely on the recurring threat of eviction to make sure rent gets paid, without ever intending to remove the tenant. The courthouse, in other words, had become the collections department.

Why landlords do it

It isn't malice, mostly. It's incentives. In much of the country, filing is cheap — in some counties under $60 — and the process moves fast. When your only built-in tool for a late payment is "file at the courthouse," you use the tool you have. A filing generates a court date, a sense of urgency, and often a payment. From a purely tactical view, it works often enough to become a habit.

But "it works often enough" is exactly the logic of a bad system. The filing isn't collecting the rent — the fear of the filing is. And fear is an expensive, unstable, one-time lever. You can't build a reliable rental business on a tool whose entire mechanism is scaring a tenant you've already decided you want to keep.

What it actually costs you

Here's the part the filing-fee receipt doesn't show. The $60 to file is the cheapest line item in the whole transaction. The real costs stack up behind it:

  • Your time. Every filing is paperwork, a court date, and the hours around it — hours a passive, well-run portfolio shouldn't require.
  • Escalation risk. A tenant you only meant to nudge may now start apartment-hunting, withholding, or lawyering up. You turned a late payment into an adversary.
  • Turnover — the big one. If the filing tips a payable tenant into actually leaving, you've traded a late check for a vacant unit, a cleanout, re-advertising, screening, and lost rent. (More on that math in our companion piece on diversion.)
  • Fees that fuel conflict. The ~$180 per filing in fines and fees mostly lands on the tenant — deepening the exact resentment and financial strain that made them late to begin with. You're adding weight to the person you need to pay you.
  • Your own record. A portfolio known for heavy filing attracts scrutiny, bad reviews, and in a growing number of cities, regulatory attention aimed squarely at serial filers.

The trap you're building

There's a deeper problem than cost. Serial filing is self-defeating because it degrades the very asset it's trying to protect: a paying, staying tenant. Every filing makes that tenant more likely to leave, more financially strained, and more adversarial — which makes the next late payment more likely, which makes the next filing more likely. You've built a flywheel that spins toward vacancy.

Policy researchers point out that the clearest ways to reduce serial filing are to make filing slower and more expensive. That tells you something: the behavior only makes sense when filing is nearly free. The moment it has a real cost, nobody does it on purpose.

So the strategic question isn't "how do I file more efficiently?" It's "what would I use instead if the courthouse weren't available?" Answer that, and you've found the system you should have been using all along.

A better collection system

A real collection system does three things the courthouse can't: it makes on-time payment the path of least resistance, it gives you leverage before a conflict instead of after, and it keeps the tenant housed and paying rather than filed-on and leaving. That's not idealism — it's just a better-designed version of what you're already trying to do when you file to collect.

The reframe You're not trying to evict. You've told the researchers as much. You're trying to get paid, on time, with the least hassle, from a tenant you'd rather keep. That is a software problem, not a courtroom problem — and it has a software solution.
