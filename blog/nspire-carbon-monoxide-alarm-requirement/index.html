NSPIRE Makes Carbon Monoxide Alarms Mandatory.
A Missing One Fails Your Inspection in 24 Hours.

Under UPCS, carbon monoxide alarms were not required in HCV properties at all. Under NSPIRE, a missing CO alarm is the highest-severity defect classification — Life-Threatening — and must be corrected within 24 hours or your HCV contract is at risk.

By Perfect Tenant Innovation  ·  September 2026  ·  8 min read

Source: HUD NSPIRE HCV/PBV Inspection Checklist (OMB 2577-0289) · HUD REAC NSPIRE Standards · 24 CFR 5.703

This is a Life-Threatening defect — the highest NSPIRE severity tier
A Life-Threatening (LT) defect under NSPIRE requires correction within 24 hours. There is no grace period, no extension, and no offsetting with other passing scores. If your unit has a missing or non-functioning CO alarm at inspection, the clock starts immediately.

Carbon monoxide is odorless, colorless, and lethal at sufficient concentrations. It is produced by fuel-burning appliances — gas furnaces, water heaters, stoves, fireplaces, generators, and attached garages. The Centers for Disease Control estimates that more than 400 Americans die from unintentional, non-fire-related CO poisoning annually, with thousands more hospitalized.

HUD's previous inspection standard — UPCS — did not require carbon monoxide alarms in Housing Choice Voucher properties. That meant Section 8 landlords were not penalized for failing to install them during HUD inspections, even in units with gas appliances or attached garages. NSPIRE changed that completely.

Under NSPIRE, a missing carbon monoxide alarm is not a Low defect or even a Moderate defect. It is Life-Threatening — the same severity tier as an exposed electrical conductor, a gas leak, a blocked exit, and a structural system in failure. The correction timeframe is 24 hours. Not 30 days. Not a re-inspection cycle. Twenty-four hours.

UPCS vs. NSPIRE on carbon monoxide alarms

Old standard — UPCS
Not required
UPCS did not include carbon monoxide alarms as an inspectable item. A unit could pass HUD inspection without a single CO alarm regardless of how many fuel-burning appliances it contained.
New standard — NSPIRE
Life-Threatening fail
A missing, obstructed, or non-functioning CO alarm is classified Life-Threatening — the highest NSPIRE severity tier. Correction required within 24 hours. No exceptions documented in the checklist.

This is one of the most significant single-item changes NSPIRE makes for landlords who have been managing Section 8 properties under UPCS standards. A requirement that did not exist at all now carries the highest penalty category in the entire inspection framework. Every Section 8 landlord who has not already installed CO alarms in applicable units is currently out of compliance with NSPIRE — and will fail their next HCV inspection on this item alone.

The 3 CO alarm conditions that each independently trigger a Life-Threatening fail

The NSPIRE HCV checklist identifies three separate deficiency conditions for carbon monoxide alarms — and each one independently classifies as Life-Threatening. You do not need all three present. Any one of them fails your unit.

LT FAIL #1
Carbon monoxide alarm is missing, not installed, or not installed in a proper location
The alarm does not exist in the unit, was never installed, has been removed, or is present but installed in a location that does not meet the proper placement standard — such as inside a closet, behind furniture, or in a location where it cannot adequately detect CO at sleeping areas. Proper location is determined by applicable state and local codes and manufacturer recommendations; typically within 10 feet of each bedroom door.
→ Life-Threatening · 24-hour correction required
LT FAIL #2
Carbon monoxide alarm is obstructed
The alarm is installed but physically blocked — by furniture, stored items, wall hangings, or any object that prevents it from detecting CO in the air or from being heard when it activates. An obstructed alarm provides no meaningful protection and is treated the same as a missing alarm under NSPIRE's Life-Threatening classification. Inspectors look for clearance around the alarm during their unit walk-through.
→ Life-Threatening · 24-hour correction required
LT FAIL #3
Carbon monoxide alarm does not produce an audio or visual alarm when tested
The alarm is present and properly located but fails the functional test. NSPIRE inspectors test CO alarms — they do not simply confirm presence. An alarm that does not produce an audible or visual signal when activated during the test is classified Life-Threatening regardless of its age, brand, or appearance. Dead batteries, failed sensors, and disconnected units all produce this result. The alarm must function, not just exist.
→ Life-Threatening · 24-hour correction required
The testing requirement is the one landlords miss most Many landlords have CO alarms installed in their Section 8 units — but have never tested them since installation. A CO alarm with dead batteries, a depleted sensor (most CO sensors have a 5–7 year lifespan), or a disconnected power source will pass a visual inspection but fail the functional test an NSPIRE inspector will conduct. Presence is not compliance. Function is compliance.

Where CO alarms must be installed — and which units require them

NSPIRE requires CO alarms to be installed in a proper location, and proper location is defined by applicable state and local law as well as HUD guidance. As a general framework, the following placement standards apply across most jurisdictions and align with NSPIRE's intent:

✓ Required — units with these sources
Fuel-burning appliances
Gas furnaces, gas water heaters, gas cooking ranges, gas fireplaces, oil-burning heating systems, and wood-burning fireplaces or stoves. Any appliance that combusts a carbon-based fuel can produce CO.
✓ Required
Attached garages
A garage physically connected to the living space — even by a single interior door — is a CO risk source. Vehicle exhaust from an attached garage can migrate into the unit quickly, especially in cold weather when cars are left running.
✓ Required
Near sleeping areas
CO alarms must be installed within 10 feet of each bedroom door at a minimum. CO poisoning is most dangerous during sleep, when it cannot be detected consciously. Bedroom-proximity placement is the baseline standard in virtually all state codes that require CO alarms.
⚠ Verify your state and local law
Check your jurisdiction's specific requirements
CO alarm placement requirements vary by state. Alabama, Tennessee, Georgia, Florida, Texas, and Ohio each have different statutory requirements. Verify your specific state's law — NSPIRE's "proper location" standard defers to applicable local code.

Multi-unit buildings: alarm placement across common areas

In multi-family buildings, CO alarms in individual units are the primary NSPIRE requirement. However, shared mechanical rooms containing fuel-burning boilers or heating systems that serve multiple units also represent CO risk points. Check with your PHA and applicable state code for shared-area requirements in your building type.

Exactly how NSPIRE inspectors evaluate CO alarms

Understanding what the inspector does during a CO alarm check helps landlords prepare their units properly — and understand why "installed" is not enough.

👁
Step 1: Visual confirmation of presence
The inspector confirms a CO alarm is physically present in the unit. If no alarm is visible, this immediately triggers the Life-Threatening deficiency for "missing, not installed, or not installed in a proper location." The inspection proceeds but the fail is already recorded.
No alarm present → LT fail recorded
📍
Step 2: Location assessment
If present, the inspector assesses whether the alarm is installed in a proper location per applicable code — not inside a closet, not behind a door, not blocked by furniture or other objects. An alarm present but improperly located triggers the first LT fail condition.
Improper location → LT fail recorded
🚧
Step 3: Obstruction check
The inspector checks whether anything obstructs the alarm — physically blocks its sensors or would prevent the alarm sound from reaching sleeping areas. Any obstruction triggers the second LT fail condition independently of location.
Obstructed alarm → LT fail recorded
🔊
Step 4: Functional test — audio or visual response
The inspector tests the alarm by pressing the test button. The alarm must produce an audio alarm, a visual alarm (flashing light), or both. If the alarm fails to respond — dead battery, depleted sensor, disconnected power — this is the third LT fail condition. An alarm that looks good but doesn't respond to the test fails identically to a missing alarm.
No audio/visual response → LT fail recorded Responds to test → passes this item
Three separate Life-Threatening fails — any one of them is enough The NSPIRE checklist lists three CO alarm deficiency conditions as separate line items, each independently rated Life-Threatening. A landlord who has a CO alarm installed but obstructed still fails. A landlord whose CO alarm is in the right place but has dead batteries still fails. All three conditions must be clear for the unit to pass this item. The 24-hour correction clock runs from the time of inspection — not from when the landlord is notified.

What happens if you don't correct within 24 hours

When a Life-Threatening defect is identified during an NSPIRE inspection, the PHA is notified immediately. The landlord has 24 hours to correct the defect and notify the PHA. If the defect is not corrected within that window, the PHA is required to take action to protect the tenant — which can include temporarily relocating the family at the owner's expense, suspending Housing Assistance Payments, or terminating the Housing Assistance Payments (HAP) contract.

For landlords with multiple HCV units, a Life-Threatening fail on one unit does not automatically affect others — but it does trigger scrutiny of your entire portfolio. PHAs have the authority to inspect all of a landlord's HCV units when a serious deficiency pattern emerges.

Action steps: bring your portfolio into CO alarm compliance today

1
Audit every HCV unit for CO alarm presence today — not before the next inspection
Walk every unit. Confirm a CO alarm is physically present, unobstructed, and within 10 feet of every sleeping area. Do this now — not the week before your next NSPIRE inspection. A missing alarm is a 24-hour fail; by the time your inspection is scheduled it's too late to prepare.
2
Test every installed alarm — press the test button and confirm response
Every CO alarm in your portfolio needs to be tested. Press the test button. Confirm you hear an audible alarm or see a visual response. If it doesn't respond, replace it immediately. If it responds weakly, replace it. CO sensor lifespan is typically 5–7 years — if you don't know when an alarm was installed, replace it.
3
Replace batteries on a fixed annual schedule — not when they die
Battery-powered CO alarms that run out of power fail the functional test identically to a unit with no alarm at all. Set a fixed annual battery replacement date — September is ideal, before heating season and before October 1 when heating requirements also kick in. Document the replacement date for every unit.
4
Install hardwired combination smoke/CO alarms in new-turnover units
When a unit turns over, install a hardwired combination smoke and CO alarm — ideally with battery backup. Hardwired alarms eliminate the battery failure risk entirely and address both the smoke alarm requirement and the CO alarm requirement in a single device. The cost difference from a battery unit is modest; the compliance benefit is permanent.
5
Document every installation, test, and replacement in writing
If a PHA disputes whether a CO alarm was present or functional, the documentation you have from your pre-inspection audit is your evidence. Record the date of installation, the brand and model, the test result, and the battery replacement date for every alarm in every unit. Store it where you can retrieve it within minutes of a PHA inquiry.
The CO alarm fix is the lowest-cost, highest-impact NSPIRE compliance step available A combination smoke/CO alarm costs $25–$60. Installing one in a unit that doesn't have one takes 15 minutes. That single step converts a Life-Threatening fail — one that can suspend your Housing Assistance Payments within 24 hours — into a clean pass on one of NSPIRE's most scrutinized items. No other NSPIRE compliance action has a better cost-to-risk ratio. If you do nothing else before your next HCV inspection, do this.
