Las Vegas, Nevada: What Happened Behind the Marquee

Behind every marquee, a neighborhood you didn't see Las Vegas's global image runs on the Strip — the lights, the names, the spectacle. What that image has never centered is the neighborhood three miles away where the city's Black entertainers, dealers, and workers were required to live, because segregation didn't let them stay in the buildings their own names were billed on. This post is about that neighborhood, the Historic Westside, and the eviction crisis it's living through today.

"The Mississippi of the West"

Las Vegas's segregation era earned it that nickname for a reason. Beginning in the 1930s, the city's hotels and casinos hired Black workers only for behind-the-scenes jobs — the public-facing roles, dealers and servers, were reserved for white employees. The exception was the headliners: Sammy Davis Jr., Nat King Cole, Ella Fitzgerald, Pearl Bailey — Black performers whose names appeared on the marquees of the same Strip casinos that would not let them walk through the front door, gamble at the tables, or rent a room for the night.

After their sets ended, those performers crossed the tracks to West Las Vegas — the neighborhood the city's Black population was restricted to living in — and stayed in boarding houses like hotelier Genevieve Harrison's, since no Strip hotel would have them. The Westside built its own answer to that exclusion: Jackson Avenue, known locally as Jackson Street, filled with Black-owned clubs, restaurants, and bars that became, for a stretch of the 1950s, a nightlife scene that rivaled the Strip itself.

The Moulin Rouge Agreement

May 24, 1955

The Moulin Rouge Hotel and Casino opens at the edge of the Westside — the first racially integrated casino in Las Vegas history, offering gambling, fine dining, and top entertainment to guests of any race.

October 1955

The Moulin Rouge closes after just five months. Its front-of-house Black workers — dancers, dealers, waiters — largely aren't hired at other casinos afterward.

March 26, 1960

Facing an NAACP-threatened march down the Strip — backed publicly by Sammy Davis Jr. and Frank Sinatra — city officials, hotel owners, and Black community leaders sign the Moulin Rouge Agreement at the defunct hotel, desegregating Las Vegas casinos.

The agreement worked, as far as it went. Black Las Vegans could now gamble, dine, and stay anywhere in the city. It's one of the more remarkable civil rights victories of its era — won through organizing pressure, at a hotel that had already failed as a business but succeeded, permanently, as a symbol.

The irony of integration

Here is the part of the Westside's story every other neighborhood in this wave doesn't share: what hollowed it out wasn't outside developers pricing residents out. It was the very victory its residents had organized for. Once desegregation gave Black Las Vegans the choice to live, work, and spend their leisure time anywhere in the city, many did — understandably. Jackson Street's clubs lost customers to the Strip they'd finally been let into. Buildings emptied. By the time the Moulin Rouge was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1992, it had sat vacant for years; an arson fire finished it off in 2003.

Decades of decline followed — boarded-up storefronts, vacant lots, a neighborhood the rest of the city had mostly stopped seeing. It's a genuinely different mechanism than studentification in Gainesville or renaissance branding in Greenville: not displacement by force, but a community's own hard-won mobility leaving behind the streets that had sheltered it when it had nowhere else to go.

The eviction numbers now

14%
Las Vegas's 2025 eviction filing rate — nearly double Eviction Lab's cross-city average of 7.9%, and higher than New York, Philadelphia, or Austin
42,596
Eviction filings in Las Vegas over a recent 12-month period, per Eviction Lab — down 20% from a year earlier, but still described by the Lab as "plateaued at really high levels"

Eviction Lab spokesperson Juan Pablo Garnham put the Las Vegas rate in blunt terms: "During 2025, landlords filed 14 evictions for every 100 renter households" — a rate the Lab noted was higher than the filing rates in New York, Philadelphia, or Austin, and still well above what the Lab sees in a normal year in places like Dallas or the entire state of Wisconsin. Nevada courts recorded 53,097 filings statewide in fiscal year 2025 — essentially flat from 53,775 the year before.

Eviction Lab's most recent national report found Black renters made up 28% of the renter population it tracks but represented 39% of eviction filings — a disparity the report calls out explicitly, and one Las Vegas eviction attorney Calder Gabroy connects to a structural imbalance in Nevada's own courts: "the judicial system is clearly weighted toward landlords in Nevada." A Nevada Independent investigation in 2023 documented a 170% surge in Las Vegas Valley evictions after pandemic-era tenant protections expired, with courtrooms filled disproportionately by women and mothers — a pattern the National Equity Atlas found falls hardest on Black, Latino, and Native American renters in Nevada, who were already the most housing-insecure groups in the state before the pandemic began.

A note on this post's sourcing City-level racial breakdowns of Las Vegas eviction filings specifically are collected by Eviction Lab through Las Vegas Review-Journal reporter Subrina Hudson's Clark County data, but a precise city-level Black-defendant percentage wasn't available at time of writing. The 39%-vs-28% figure above is Eviction Lab's most recent national estimate across all 38 tracked cities, cited here as the best available evidence of the pattern, not a Las Vegas-specific number.

The pressure today

The Historic Westside now faces a second kind of pressure — not the decline that followed desegregation, but renewed development interest tied to its proximity to downtown Las Vegas. Claytee White, director of UNLV's Oral History Research Center, has said the neighborhood's location may lead to "gentrification and additional displacement of existing residents." Chandler Cooks, who is set to inherit a four-plex and a house on Jackson Avenue from his grandmother, described the challenge in plain terms at a 2024 city listening session: inflation, his own student loan debt, gentrification pressure, and what he called the "trickle down effects" of decades of redlining, all converging on the same handful of blocks his family has held onto.

Two revitalization efforts are underway now — one led by community organizers, one by the City of Las Vegas — sometimes aligned, sometimes colliding, according to reporting by The Nevada Independent, with residents wary of a city that historically kept its distance from the neighborhood suddenly taking an interest in it.
