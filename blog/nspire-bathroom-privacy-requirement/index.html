Your Bathroom Must Have Privacy.
That's a HUD Requirement.

Under HUD's NSPIRE standards, the toilet and the tub or shower in your home must be usable in private. It sounds basic, but a missing or broken bathroom door can fail an inspection. Here's what the rule means, plus the full checklist of what your bathroom is required to have.

By Perfect Tenant Innovation  ·  September 2026  ·  6 min read

Source: 24 CFR 5.703(d) · HUD NSPIRE HCV Inspection Checklist (OMB 2577-0289) · HUD REAC NSPIRE Standards

The privacy rule Real examples Full bathroom checklist What to do

Most of HUD's NSPIRE inspection standards are about things that are broken or dangerous, like no heat, exposed wiring, or mold. But NSPIRE also includes a short list of things every home must have to count as livable. HUD calls these Affirmative Habitability Requirements, and one of them is simple: you have to be able to use the bathroom in private.

For most renters, this is never an issue, because the bathroom has a door that closes. But doors get broken, removed, or never replaced, and some homes have unusual layouts. When that happens, the privacy rule gives you a clear standard to point to.

The privacy rule

🚪 Under 24 CFR 5.703(d)
The toilet and the bathtub or shower must be usable in private.
In practice, that usually means a bathroom door that closes. If you can't use the toilet or bathe with privacy, it's a Moderate defect your landlord must fix within 30 days once an inspection documents it.

Privacy is part of dignity, and it's part of safety too, especially for families with children or several adults sharing a home. HUD treats it as a baseline requirement, not a luxury.

Real examples

🚫
The bathroom door was removed and never replaced.

Fails · 30 days

A standard bathroom with a working door that closes.
Meets the rule
When it's unclear, ask The rule focuses on being able to use the bathroom privately, and some situations are less clear-cut, like a broken lock or a bathroom you can only reach through another bedroom. Your Housing Authority decides how the standard applies in your home. If something doesn't feel private, report it and ask.

The full bathroom checklist

Privacy is one part of the picture. NSPIRE expects every bathroom to have these basics. If any are missing or broken, it's generally a 30-day fix.

What to do if your bathroom falls short

1
Take photos
Photograph the missing or broken door, the fixture that doesn't work, or the fan that won't turn on. Your phone records the date automatically.
2
Report it in writing
Send your landlord a text, email, or message describing the problem and when it started. For privacy issues, say plainly that the bathroom can't be used in private.
3
Follow up, then call your Housing Authority
If nothing happens within a few days, follow up in writing. If it's still not fixed, contact your PHA and ask for an inspection. Once an inspector documents the problem, the 30-day deadline applies.
4
Don't take matters into your own hands
Don't withhold rent without legal advice, since that can lead to eviction. And don't remove or alter doors yourself. Under program rules, families are responsible for damage they cause.

[Your name] · [Phone]
The bottom line You have a right to use your bathroom in private, and to a bathroom with a working toilet, a tub or shower, a sink, hot and cold water, and ventilation. If yours falls short, photograph it, report it in writing, and bring in your Housing Authority if it isn't fixed.
📞
Need help?
For problems with your unit, contact your Public Housing Authority. For help finding local housing or legal assistance, call 211, the free 24/7 helpline. For emergencies, call 911.
Source: The requirement that the toilet and bathtub or shower be usable in private is one of the Affirmative Habitability Requirements under 24 CFR 5.703(d). Bathroom deficiency classifications are drawn from the HUD NSPIRE HCV/PBV Inspection Checklist (OMB No. 2577-0289), available at hud.gov, and HUD's NSPIRE standards at hud.gov/reac/nspire. How the privacy standard applies to specific layouts is determined by your PHA. Family responsibility for household-caused damage is set out in 24 CFR Part 982.
