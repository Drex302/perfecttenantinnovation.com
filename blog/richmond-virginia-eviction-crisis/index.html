Richmond Has the Second-Highest Eviction Rate in America.
Researchers Say Race — Not Poverty — Is Why.

One in four Richmond renters faced an eviction filing in 2025. Virginia holds five cities in the national top ten. And researchers at Virginia Commonwealth University found that the single most decisive factor driving evictions in Richmond's neighborhoods isn't income. It's race.

There is a county in Virginia — Dickenson County — with nearly the same poverty rate as the City of Richmond. Its eviction rate is below 1%. Its population is more than 95% white.

Richmond's population is nearly 50% Black. Its eviction rate in 2025 was 24% — one filing for every four renter households, the second-highest rate of any city in the United States.

Researchers at Virginia Commonwealth University studied this divergence. They controlled for income. They controlled for property values. They controlled for every economic variable they could measure. And they found that the single most influential factor determining eviction risk in a Richmond neighborhood — more than poverty, more than rent burden, more than any economic indicator — was race.

The neighborhoods with more Black residents had higher eviction rates. The neighborhoods with more white residents had lower ones. The income levels were similar. The outcome was not.

"The most influential factor at the neighborhood level on eviction rates is the racial composition of the neighborhood. As the neighborhood has a higher share of Black and African-American residents, it also has a higher eviction rate — and conversely, if the neighborhood is whiter, it has a lower eviction rate."

— Benjamin Teresa, Ph.D., Associate Professor of Urban and Regional Studies, Virginia Commonwealth University

Virginia doesn't have an eviction problem. It has five of them.

This is not a coincidence. It is a pattern with a history. And understanding that history is inseparable from understanding why Black renters in Virginia face eviction at rates that dwarf their share of the renting population.

The line from redlining to eviction court: a century of policy in five steps

Richmond's eviction geography does not exist in a vacuum. It maps almost exactly onto the city's redlining maps — the color-coded federal assessments from the 1930s that designated Black neighborhoods as "hazardous" investment risks, cutting them off from mortgage financing, capital investment, and the wealth-building opportunities that homeownership provided to white families for generations. That history is not ancient. Its effects are present in who owns property in Richmond today, who rents, and who ends up in eviction court.

1930s
Federal redlining maps drawn in Richmond and across Virginia
The Home Owners Loan Corporation grades Richmond's neighborhoods by race — Black communities marked "hazardous" in red, cutting them off from FHA mortgage access. White families access federally-backed homeownership. Black families are locked out. The wealth gap begins compounding immediately.
1940s–70s
Disinvestment, urban renewal, and displacement in redlined neighborhoods
Decades of disinvestment in formerly redlined areas — combined with highway construction that severed Black communities and urban renewal programs that destroyed them — collapse property values and force Black residents into the rental market as homeownership paths narrow. The renter concentration in historically Black Richmond neighborhoods deepens.
1980s–2010s
Corporate and institutional landlords acquire rental stock in Black neighborhoods
As real estate investment firms identify formerly redlined neighborhoods as undervalued acquisition targets, large corporate landlords buy up rental stock at scale. The Breeden Company alone filed 708 evictions in Hampton Roads in the first nine months of 2024 — nearly twice the next-highest filer. When housing is owned by volume landlords optimizing for cash flow, eviction filing becomes a management strategy rather than a last resort.
2019–2023
Rents surge 30%+ statewide while Black household income growth stalls
Corporate and predatory landlords raise rents by over 30% across Virginia between 2019 and 2024 — in some locations, 40–50% in a single year. In majority-Black neighborhoods already carrying the weight of generations of disinvestment, rents rising faster than incomes is not an abstraction. It is the math that creates an eviction filing.
2025
Richmond: 24% eviction rate. Black renters: 18.6% of Virginia renters, 50%+ of filings.
The endpoint of a century of compounding policy. In Virginia, 60% of majority-Black neighborhoods have eviction rates above 10% — four times the national average. The eviction court is where the redlining map meets the present tense.
The Dickenson County comparison — what it proves Dickenson and Cannon counties in rural Virginia have nearly identical poverty rates to the City of Richmond. Their eviction rates are below 1%. Both counties are more than 95% white. This natural experiment — same poverty, radically different eviction outcomes — is what led VCU researchers to conclude that race, not income, is the decisive variable. The economic conditions don't explain the gap. The racial composition of the neighborhood does.

What drives Richmond's eviction rate: four compounding factors

1
Virginia's 5-day pay-or-quit notice — faster than most of the country
Virginia requires landlords to give tenants five days to pay or vacate before filing for eviction for non-payment. While slightly longer than Georgia's 3-day notice, five days is still among the shortest in the country and leaves little runway for a renter hit by an unexpected income disruption to organize payment. For a household already living paycheck to paycheck in a neighborhood with rising rents, five days is not enough time to solve a $900 shortfall.
2
Corporate landlord concentration and high-volume filing practices
Eviction Lab data consistently shows that a small number of landlords are responsible for a disproportionate share of eviction filings. In Hampton Roads, a single property management company filed 708 evictions in nine months. In Marion County, Indiana, 100 properties produced one-third of all habitability complaints. This pattern — concentrated landlord power using eviction filing as a routine management tool rather than a last resort — runs through every high-eviction city in the nation, and Richmond is no exception.
3
No right to counsel in eviction court
Virginia law does not guarantee tenants the right to an attorney in eviction proceedings. Research consistently shows that tenants with legal representation have dramatically better outcomes — more cases dismissed, more payment plans agreed to, fewer wrongful evictions. Tenants without representation often don't know their rights, don't challenge defective notices, and default without contest. The disparity in legal representation in Richmond's eviction courts tracks closely with race and income.
4
Eviction records that follow renters indefinitely
In Virginia, eviction court records are public and permanent. Unlike some states that seal eviction filings — particularly when a case is dismissed or the tenant pays — Virginia's records are accessible to background check services indefinitely. A single eviction filing, even one that never resulted in a judgment, can prevent a renter from being approved for housing for years. The "Scarlet E," as Eviction Lab researchers call it, turns a one-time housing crisis into a permanent barrier to stable housing.

What it would take to fix this — and what you can do while policy catches up

Housing advocates and researchers have identified the policy changes that would reduce Virginia's eviction rates: lengthening the notice period beyond five days, providing a right to counsel in eviction court, sealing eviction records for dismissed cases and paid judgments, and implementing source-of-income protections that prevent landlords from screening out voucher holders. Other states and cities have implemented versions of these protections. New York City's universal access to legal counsel is credited by researchers with keeping its eviction rate at 5% — compared to Richmond's 24%.

Those changes require sustained political pressure, organized advocacy, and time. The families displaced in 2025's 24% filing rate did not have that time.
