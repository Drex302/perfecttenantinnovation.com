Houston, Texas: The Eviction That Never Comes Off Your Record

Houston tells itself it's the city where anybody can make it. Its eviction filing rate is nearly double New York's — and in Texas, the filing follows you forever, even if you win.

The detail that makes Houston different In most cities, an eviction case that you win, settle, or pay off can eventually be sealed — wiped from the record so it doesn't haunt your next apartment application. In Texas, it generally can't. According to the Houston Eviction Advocacy Center, eviction records in Texas typically cannot be sealed or removed even if a tenant later resolves the case. That single fact turns every one of Harris County's 75,000-plus annual filings into something closer to permanent. This post is about what that does to a community.

The permanent record

A Kinder Institute researcher at Rice University named the trap precisely: "Regardless of whether you win the case or not, that might follow you forever." In Houston, the filing itself is the damage. You don't have to lose. You don't have to be wrong. A landlord filing against a tenant who was, in one attorney's account, literally at the bank pulling the money to pay — minutes from resolving everything — still creates a court record that generally cannot be erased in Texas.

That record then does its own quiet work for years. As the Kinder Institute's Stephen Sherman put it, an eviction filing can linger on a credit score even after it's overturned; a landlord seeing it on a background check "may choose to raise the rent" or simply decline to rent at all. The filing becomes a tax the tenant keeps paying long after the dispute is over — a scarlet letter issued by a court, in a state that offers almost no way to take it back off.

The eviction numbers

75,000+
Eviction filings across Harris County in 2025, per Rice University's Kinder Institute — one of the highest volumes of any metro in the country
~2x
Houston's eviction filing rate relative to New York City's, per the Houston Chronicle — despite Houston being far smaller

Eviction Lab's 2025 report counted roughly 77,000 filings in the Houston area — a figure that, even after a 5% year-over-year drop, still dwarfs entire states: it's more eviction cases than the Lab recorded in New Mexico, Minnesota, Connecticut, Rhode Island, Delaware, and Wisconsin combined. Texas consistently ranks among the highest-volume eviction states in the nation, in large part because its cases move through the courts fast and cheap for landlords to file.

The county average runs about 9 eviction filings per 100 renter households, but that average hides enormous variation. In one Harris County neighborhood — the FM 1960 / Cypress Creek North area of Spring — the Kinder Institute found a rate of roughly 28 filings per 100 renters, meaning nearly 30% of renters there received an eviction notice in a single year, about triple the county average. Some tracts run even higher.

Why the filing rate matters more than the "win" Houston's crisis is a case study in why this wave measures filings, not just removals. A family can keep their home and still carry the wound: the credit hit, the background-check flag, the higher rent the next landlord charges because the record is there. In a state that won't seal the record, prevention isn't just better than cure — it's very nearly the only cure available.

Sunnyside: Houston's "Black Wall Street"

To see what a permanent-record eviction crisis does to a specific community, go to Sunnyside — a historically Black neighborhood in south Houston with a history that reads like a monument to Black self-determination, and a present that reads like a warning.

1912

Sunnyside is platted and chartered as a Black community in what was then rural land outside Houston — the same year, notably, as several of the Great Migration–era Black settlements this wave has traced in other cities.

1950s–1960s

Residents build their own water district and a $1 million water-and-sewer project serving 2,000+ homes. A stretch of Cullen Boulevard becomes so dense with Black-owned businesses that residents call it "Black Wall Street."

1970s–1990s

Sunnyside loses roughly 30% of its population in a single decade as disinvestment sets in — the long hollowing that precedes today's displacement pressure.

Today

Sunnyside sits among the Houston neighborhoods the Kinder Institute identifies as actively gentrifying — and among the areas New America's housing-loss analysis flags for the county's highest eviction rates.

Just northeast of downtown, the Scenic Woods neighborhood tells the sharpest version of the story. New America's analysis of Harris County housing loss found one Scenic Woods census tract with an average eviction rate of 36% — the county's second-hardest-hit tract. Its median household income is $25,313, roughly two-fifths of the county average, and it is two-thirds Black. That is what the intersection of race, poverty, and a permanent-record eviction system looks like at the level of a single set of blocks.

Where it concentrates

The Kinder Institute's mapping found that Houston's eviction crisis is genuinely widespread — inner city, suburban, and middle-suburb neighborhoods all appear in the data. But New America's deeper analysis found that the areas with the highest housing loss cluster in predictable places: the majority-Black neighborhoods to the northeast and directly south of downtown — Southeast Houston, Sunnyside, and South Acres / Crestmont Park. Areas with concentrations of eviction filings, the Kinder Institute's dashboard partners at Texas Housers noted, also had higher shares of renters of color.

These are the same neighborhoods — Third Ward, Fifth Ward, Independence Heights (the first Black city in Texas, chartered 1905), Sunnyside — that Houston's own researchers identify as its historically Black core, now facing displacement not through a single dramatic force but through a stack of them: rising rents, rising property-tax assessments on longtime owners, hurricanes and freezes that wipe out savings, and an eviction system with no eraser.

"If one thing, one emergency, happens, you're going right underneath that water." — Kendra London, forced out of Houston's Fifth Ward in 2019 by rents that took more than half her paycheck, now an advocate for affordable housing so displaced residents can return

Who's filing

One factor the Kinder Institute could connect to the highest-filing hotspots was the presence of larger, often corporate landlords. Texas Housers' analysis of the top-evicting properties in Harris County found that the top 30 evicting properties alone accounted for nearly 1 in every 13 evictions filed county-wide — and that one in three of those top-evicting properties is owned by a non-Texan company. In at least two cases, top-evicting properties were receiving state or federal affordable-housing subsidies even as they filed among the most evictions in the county.

It's a pattern this wave has seen before: eviction filing deployed not as a last resort but as a routine business process, often by out-of-state owners with no stake in the neighborhood beyond its yield. The filing is cheap, fast, and — in Texas — permanent for the tenant. For the landlord, it's a line item.

Where PTI fits

Perfect Tenant Innovation can't change Texas law on sealing records — that's a fight for the Houston Eviction Advocacy Center, Lone Star Legal Aid, and the tenant advocates already in those JP courtrooms. But in a state where the filing is the permanent wound, the entire game is keeping the shortfall from ever becoming a filing. That's exactly what PTI is built for: rewarding on-time payment, building tenant credit through rent, and giving a landlord a documented reason to accept a payment plan — in writing, as the advocacy center advises — instead of filing a case that will follow a family for the rest of their lives.

Sunnyside built its own water system in the 1950s because no one else would. The neighborhoods around it are still building. PTI is one more tool for making sure a single bad month doesn't leave a permanent mark on people who have already built so much.
