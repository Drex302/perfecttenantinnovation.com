Mold in Your Home? Your Landlord Has to Fix It.
Here's How NSPIRE Grades It.

Under HUD's NSPIRE standards, mold inside your unit is an inspection failure, and how much there is decides how fast it must be fixed. Moderate and high mold are 30-day fixes. Extremely high mold is a 24-hour fix. Even dampness with no visible mold counts.

By Perfect Tenant Innovation  ·  September 2026  ·  8 min read

Source: HUD NSPIRE HCV Inspection Checklist (OMB 2577-0289) · HUD REAC NSPIRE Standards · U.S. EPA mold guidance · CDC

The mold levels Why it matters Where to look You vs. your landlord What to do Habits that help

Mold is one of the most common problems renters deal with, and one of the most frustrating. It shows up on the bathroom ceiling, around windows, under the sink. You wipe it away and it comes back. Sometimes a landlord paints over it, and a few weeks later it's back again.

That cycle happens because mold is a symptom. The real problem is moisture: a leak, a bathroom with no working fan, water coming in through a window or roof. HUD's NSPIRE inspection standards recognize this. They grade mold by how much there is, and they also count dampness itself as a problem, even before any mold appears. This post explains how that works and how to get the source fixed, not just the stain.

How NSPIRE grades mold inside your unit

Inspectors look at mold visually and sort it into levels. Inside your unit, here's what each level means:

💧
Dampness, no visible mold
Walls, floors, or ceilings with elevated moisture, like soft drywall, water stains, or bubbling paint.
Moderate · 30 days
🟡
Moderate mold
A contained patch, like a spot on a bathroom ceiling or around a window frame.
Moderate · 30 days
🟠
High mold
Larger growth that has spread across a bigger area or more than one surface.
Severe · 30 days
🔴
Extremely high mold
Heavy growth covering large sections of walls, ceilings, or rooms.
Life-Threatening · 24 hours
Dampness counts on its own You don't have to wait for mold to grow before reporting a problem. Under NSPIRE, elevated moisture is a failure by itself. A water stain spreading on the ceiling or a wall that feels soft is reason enough to report it now, before it turns into mold.

Why mold matters for your health

🫁 What the CDC says
Damp, moldy homes are linked to breathing problems. People with asthma, allergies, or weakened immune systems are most affected, and so are young children. Common reactions include:
Stuffy nose Wheezing Coughing Itchy or red eyes Skin irritation Asthma flare-ups

If someone in your home has breathing problems that get worse at home and better elsewhere, mention it to their doctor. And tell your landlord in your written report. It adds urgency and belongs in the record.

Where to look

🚿
Bathroom
Ceiling above the shower, grout, caulk, around the toilet base.
🪟
Windows
Sills, frames, and the wall just below, where condensation collects.
🚰
Under sinks
Cabinet floors and back walls near pipe connections.
🌀
Around vents
Air vents, and near the AC unit if it drips.
🧱
Ceilings and corners
Stains below bathrooms, roofs, or upstairs units.
👃
Musty smell
A lasting earthy smell can mean hidden mold in walls or under floors.

What you can handle, and what's your landlord's job

The EPA says small areas of mold, generally less than about 10 square feet (roughly a 3-by-3-foot patch), can usually be cleaned by the people who live there. Anything bigger, or anything caused by a leak, belongs to your landlord.

You can usually handle
  • Small surface spots on tile, tubs, or window sills
  • Cleaning with soap and water, then drying the area
  • Wiping away condensation
  • Running the bathroom fan and cracking a window
Your landlord must handle
  • Leaks from pipes, roofs, windows, or upstairs units
  • Broken or missing bathroom exhaust fans
  • Mold larger than about 10 square feet
  • Mold inside walls, ceilings, or the HVAC system
  • Damaged drywall, flooring, or ceiling
Cleaning safely If you clean a small spot, wear gloves and keep the area ventilated. Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners, since that creates toxic fumes. If anyone in your home has asthma or a weakened immune system, it's safer to let the landlord handle it. And report the spot even after you clean it, because it will come back if the moisture source isn't fixed.
Painting over mold isn't a fix If your landlord paints over mold without fixing the leak or ventilation problem causing it, the mold will come back, often worse. Under NSPIRE, the problem is the condition, not the stain. If you see it return, photograph it and report it again in writing.

What to do if you find mold or dampness

1
Photograph it, with something for scale
Take clear, dated photos. Put a ruler, coin, or your hand next to it so the size is obvious. Take another photo every week or two to show whether it's spreading.
2
Look for the source
Note anything that might be causing it, like a drip under the sink, a stain below a bathroom, or a fan that doesn't work. Include it in your report so the landlord fixes the cause.
3
Report it in writing
Send your landlord a text, email, or message describing where it is, how big it is, when you noticed it, and any leak or ventilation problem you think is behind it. Mention any health effects in your household.
4
Follow up, then call your Housing Authority
If nothing happens within a few days, follow up in writing. If it's still not addressed, contact your PHA and ask for an inspection. For heavy growth covering large areas, call right away, since that level is a 24-hour fix.
5
Don't withhold rent
Stopping rent on your own can lead to an eviction filing, and rules vary by state. The stronger tool is your PHA, which can stop its payments to the landlord once an inspection confirms the problem and the deadline passes. Talk to legal aid before withholding anything.

Everyday habits that help prevent mold

Mold is mostly a building problem, but a few habits reduce everyday moisture. They also protect you, since program rules hold families responsible for damage they cause.

  • Run the bathroom fan during showers and for about 20 minutes after. If it doesn't work, report it.
  • Use the kitchen vent fan or crack a window when cooking or boiling water.
  • Wipe up condensation on windows, especially in winter.
  • Report drips and leaks the day you notice them. Small leaks become big mold problems.
  • Dry wet towels and laundry fully, and avoid drying clothes indoors when you can.
  • Leave a little space between furniture and outside walls so air can move.
The bottom line Mold and dampness inside your unit are real inspection failures under NSPIRE, and your landlord has to fix the source, not just cover the stain. Photograph it with something for scale, report it in writing along with the cause you suspect, follow up, and bring in your Housing Authority if nothing happens.
📞
Need help?
If someone has serious trouble breathing, call 911. For problems with your unit, contact your Public Housing Authority. For help finding local housing or legal assistance, call 211, the free 24/7 helpline.
