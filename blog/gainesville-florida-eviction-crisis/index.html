Gainesville Is a College Town.
Its Black Renters Are Absorbing 53% of Eviction Filings at Just 24% of the Renter Population.

The community absorbing the most pressure is not the student population the University of Florida was built to serve. It's the historically Black neighborhoods that existed before the university dominated the city's geography.

Porters Quarters is one of the oldest Black neighborhoods in Florida. Founded in 1884 by Canadian physician Dr. Watson Porter — who specifically sold real estate only to African Americans, creating a protected Black enclave in post-Reconstruction Gainesville — it survived Jim Crow, urban renewal, and decades of disinvestment. What it is fighting now is something different: luxury student housing developments with nearly a thousand beds, rising rents driven by University of Florida enrollment growth, and a city housing market increasingly organized around serving students rather than the long-term community members who built the neighborhood's history.

Princeton University's Eviction Lab data shows what that pressure looks like in the courthouse. In 2025, Black renters were 24% of the Gainesville area renter population but 53% of eviction filing defendants — a 29-point racial gap that is among the most extreme in the Eviction Lab's entire 48-city tracking network. Gainesville's eviction filing rate has run 46% above pre-pandemic baseline levels, one of the largest sustained surges of any tracked American city.

The story of Gainesville's eviction crisis is, at its core, the story of what happens when a university town's housing market is optimized for a transient student population and the long-term Black community absorbs the cost.

The neighborhood that predates the university — and is being displaced by it

📍 Porters Quarters, East Gainesville — Founded 1884
The historically Black neighborhood that existed before the University of Florida dominated Gainesville's identity
Porters Quarters was established in 1884 — seventeen years before the University of Florida opened its doors in Gainesville. It was founded specifically as a protected space where Black residents could own property and build community in post-Reconstruction Florida. For more than a century, it anchored East Gainesville's Black community through segregation, urban renewal, and economic pressures that dismantled comparable neighborhoods in other Southern cities.

Today, Porters Quarters sits at the intersection of two converging pressures. To its west, luxury student housing development — including a 1,000-bed complex that community members sued the city to stop in 2020 — is converting the neighborhood's edges into student residential territory, raising surrounding property values and rents beyond what longtime residents can afford. Residents described to the Independent Florida Alligator in 2024 that new construction began in their neighborhood without communication from the city. "It hurts to have to resist the city," one resident said. This is a neighborhood that has been resisting since 1884. The difference now is what it's resisting against.

What "studentification" does to a Black community

Housing researchers use the term "studentification" to describe the process by which university-adjacent neighborhoods become dominated by student residential occupation — a process that is not neutral in its effects on existing residents. In Gainesville, where the University of Florida enrolls more than 55,000 students and continues to grow, studentification is not a hypothetical risk. It is the documented experience of East Gainesville's historically Black neighborhoods.

What studentification brings
For the student housing market
  • Luxury apartment complexes with amenities priced for student budgets subsidized by family income
  • Rising property values as investment capital targets university-adjacent land
  • New retail, restaurants, and services oriented toward student demographics
  • Development pressure that converts single-family homes to multi-unit student rentals
What studentification takes
From existing Black communities
  • Affordable long-term rental units converted to student housing at premium rates
  • Displacement as rising rents exceed the income of service-sector and working-class Black households
  • Loss of community institutions — churches, small businesses, neighborhood anchors — as demographics shift
  • Eviction pressure as landlords realize they can earn more from students than from long-term Black tenants
The zoning reform debate that revealed the tension In 2022, Gainesville became the first Florida city to end single-family zoning — a move that housing advocates praised as a tool for creating more affordable supply. But community organizers from historically Black neighborhoods in East Gainesville saw a different risk: that upzoning would accelerate the conversion of their neighborhoods into higher-density student housing, speeding displacement rather than preventing it. "You're taking away so much from the Black and brown community," said Pastor Earnestine Butler of the Duval Heights area. The debate exposed what Gainesville's eviction data confirms: in a university town, housing policy decisions are rarely neutral between the student market and the long-term Black community.

Why Gainesville's eviction filing rate is 46% above pre-pandemic baseline: four factors

1
University growth driving rents beyond what long-term Black residents can afford
The University of Florida's continued enrollment growth — and the associated demand for student housing within walking or cycling distance of campus — has steadily driven up rents in East Gainesville neighborhoods adjacent to campus. Rents that were accessible to service workers, healthcare aides, and long-term community members have shifted toward student market pricing. The households who can no longer pay the new rents are the same households disproportionately represented in eviction filings.
2
Florida's 3-day eviction notice — among the shortest in the nation
Florida requires only three days' notice before a landlord can file for eviction for non-payment — tied with Georgia and Texas for the shortest notice period in PTI's markets. For a Gainesville service worker whose income varies week to week, three days is a nearly impossible window for resolving a rent gap before it becomes a legal filing. Florida's legal framework gives Gainesville's landlords the fastest possible path to the courthouse.
3
Eviction filing as a displacement mechanism in a gentrifying rental market
When landlords in gentrifying neighborhoods recognize that student renters will pay more than long-term community residents, eviction filing becomes a mechanism for tenant turnover rather than a last resort for non-payment. Research on studentification in university towns documents that landlords use formal and informal eviction processes to clear existing long-term tenants in favor of higher-paying student occupants. Gainesville's 46% above-baseline filing rate and its 29-point racial eviction gap are consistent with this pattern.
4
No source-of-income protection in Florida law
Florida has no statewide law requiring landlords to accept Housing Choice Vouchers. Gainesville landlords who prefer student renters — who typically have guarantors, family financial support, and short lease terms — can screen out voucher holders and long-term low-income tenants entirely. The concentration of eviction filings in East Gainesville's historically Black neighborhoods, where voucher use is highest, reflects both this screening and the displacement pressure that follows when affordable options shrink.

What would change this — and what Gainesville's Black community is already doing

The policy changes that would reduce Gainesville's eviction disparity: source-of-income protection requiring landlords to accept vouchers (Florida currently prohibits municipalities from enacting this), a longer notice period beyond three days, legal representation in eviction court, and anti-displacement protections for historically Black neighborhoods that constrain the conversion of long-term affordable units to student housing.

What Gainesville's Black community is already doing: Porters Quarters residents are organizing against displacement. Community members filed suit to stop the 1,000-bed luxury development. Faye Williams and her neighbors are documenting the neighborhood's history during Black History Month and beyond. These are not passive communities waiting for policy to save them. They are active communities that have been fighting for 140 years and are fighting still.
