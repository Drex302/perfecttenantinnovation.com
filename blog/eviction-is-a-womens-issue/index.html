Eviction Is a Women's Issue

Across eighteen cities, one pattern held in every single one: women are filed against more than men, and Black women with children are the most evicted group in America. This is why.

By Perfect Tenant Innovation  ·  Eviction Crisis Wave  ·  Sourced from Princeton University's Eviction Lab, Matthew Desmond's research, and the ACLU

The Pattern We Kept Seeing The National Numbers Why Women The Devil's Bargain Homes and Freedom Where PTI Fits
What eighteen cities had in common This wave traveled from Atlanta to Oklahoma City, documenting the eviction crisis one place at a time. The cities differed in almost everything — the industries, the histories, the specific machinery of displacement. But one demographic fact surfaced in city after city, unprompted, in the data itself: women are filed against at rates far above their share of renters. In Las Vegas, courtrooms filled with women and mothers. In Oklahoma City, women were 46% of filings. In Columbus, the gap landed on women and people of color. It was never a coincidence. Eviction in America is structurally gendered — and this post is about why.

The pattern we kept seeing

Go back through this series and the gender thread is impossible to miss once you're looking for it:

The women's numbers, city by city

  • Oklahoma City: women made up 46% of all eviction filings, though they're 52% of renters — but Black women specifically were filed on far above their share.
  • Las Vegas: a Nevada Independent investigation found post-pandemic eviction courtrooms disproportionately filled by women and mothers.
  • Columbus: the boom's evictions landed disproportionately on Black, Latino, and women renters.
  • Phoenix: the woman whose story opened the crisis — newly homeless at nearly 60 after a single eviction — was one of countless women the fast-filing system caught.
  • Milwaukee & the Eviction Belt: the original research ground where this pattern was first measured.

These weren't separate stories. They were the same national structure showing up locally, again and again. When you assemble the national data that sits underneath all of them, the picture is unambiguous.

The national numbers

In 2023, Princeton's Eviction Lab did something it had never been able to do before: by linking 38 million eviction court cases to U.S. Census Bureau records, it identified the demographics of evicted Americans by race, gender, and age — data that eviction court filings themselves never captured, because they only list names on a lease. The finding was stark. The single demographic most at risk of eviction in the United States is Black women — and Black women with children most of all. They make up 28.3% of the average annual rate of eviction filings, the highest of any group measured.

This built on Desmond's earlier, landmark finding from Milwaukee: while Black renters overall faced the highest eviction rates, Black and Latina women faced higher rates than the men in their own communities. Over 50% of all eviction filings in this country are against a Black family. Layer gender on top of that, and Black women sit at the exact intersection of the two largest disparities in the entire American eviction system.

Why women

The gendering of eviction isn't mysterious. It's the predictable product of three forces stacking on top of one another — each of which this wave touched in individual cities, and all of which converge on the same households.

1. The wage gap

Women earn less than men for the same work, and Black women experience one of the widest wage gaps of any demographic group in the country. When rent consumes half or more of a household's income — as it does for most low-income renting families in America — a smaller paycheck means a thinner margin, and a thinner margin means a single missed payment is more likely. Lower pay doesn't just mean less money; in a housing market this tight, it means less protection against the exact shortfall that triggers a filing.

2. Single motherhood

As Desmond put it bluntly, "the face of the American eviction epidemic is moms with kids." Women, and especially Black women, disproportionately head single-parent households — carrying the full rent on one income while raising children. Nearly half of Black youth live with their mothers in a single-parent household. A two-earner household that loses one income has another to fall back on; a single mother who loses hers has nothing between her and the courthouse. The households least cushioned against a bad month are disproportionately headed by women.

3. Nuisance ordinances

This is the cruelest of the three, and the least known. Many cities have "nuisance property" ordinances that penalize landlords when police are called to a property too many times — including calls for domestic violence. Desmond's analysis of two years of Milwaukee nuisance citations found that in roughly 83% of domestic-violence nuisance cases, landlords responded by evicting or threatening to evict the tenant. Most of the time, the person evicted was the woman being abused — often by a man who didn't even live on the lease.

The devil's bargain

That third force produces what Desmond named a "devil's bargain," and it is the sharpest illustration of why eviction is a women's issue in a way it simply is not a men's issue. A woman being abused faces an impossible choice: call the police and risk having her landlord evict her for generating a "nuisance," or stay silent and endure the abuse to keep her home.

Women are forced to choose between reporting domestic violence and keeping a roof over their heads. Every few days, somewhere in a city with a nuisance ordinance, a woman loses that bargain.

Housing instability and gender-based violence turn out to be the same crisis wearing two faces. Researchers have found that improving women's economic conditions — closing the wage gap specifically — is associated with reductions in domestic violence itself. Housing stability isn't downstream of women's safety; it's part of the same structure. A woman who cannot be evicted for calling 911 is a woman who can call 911.

Homes and freedom

Matthew Desmond, whose Evicted defined the modern understanding of this crisis, compressed the whole argument into a single sentence that has become the defining line of the field:

"Poor black men are locked up. Poor black women are locked out."

Eviction is to women what incarceration is to men — the systematic, racialized removal from home and community that shapes the life of an entire population. It carries the same permanence this wave documented in Houston and Oklahoma City: an eviction record bars women from future housing and from affordable-housing programs, strips them of possessions, and follows them for years. And it lands hardest on the women with the least margin to absorb it — single mothers, domestic-violence survivors, Black women earning the lowest wages for the same work.

To call eviction a women's issue is not to minimize how it harms men, or families, or communities as a whole. It is to name accurately where the weight falls hardest, so that the solutions can be aimed there.

Where PTI fits

Perfect Tenant Innovation can't close the wage gap or repeal a nuisance ordinance — those are fights for legislators, organizers, and advocates, and they're essential. But the single-missed-payment shortfall that the wage gap and single motherhood make more likely is exactly the moment PTI is built to intervene. By rewarding on-time payment, building a woman's credit through the rent she's already paying, and giving landlords a documented reason to accept a payment plan instead of filing, PTI works to keep the thinnest-margin households — disproportionately women, disproportionately mothers — out of the courthouse in the first place.

A housing system that evicts women for calling the police, and files against mothers at the highest rate of any group in the country, is not a neutral system. Building tools that protect the households it targets hardest is not neutral either — it's the point.
