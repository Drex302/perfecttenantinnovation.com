Texas Tenant Rights Guide (2026)

Texas residential leasing is mostly governed by the Texas Property Code Chapter 92 — but not every unit or landlord is covered the same way. This page is an educational orientation for renters in Houston, DFW, Austin, San Antonio, and statewide: deposits, serious repair issues, illegal lockouts, eviction flow, and where to get real legal help.

Texas tenant rights: the big picture

Compared with many coastal states, Texas is often described as relatively landlord-favorable on pricing and termination mechanics: there is no statewide rent control, and non-payment cases frequently start from a short written notice-to-vacate period that is commonly discussed as three days for rent default (check your lease — it may give you more time).

Where Texas still gives tenants meaningful leverage: deposit accounting and bad-faith retention (for covered leases), illegal lockout and utility interruption remedies, a structured process for certain health-and-safety repairs, anti-retaliation rules in defined situations, and specific early-termination rights (for example, certain family violence protections and federal military protections).

Chapter 92 does not cover every rental
Small-landlord and single-family exemptions exist (for example, certain owners with limited numbers of dwelling units). If an exemption applies, different deposit and notice rules may apply. Do not assume this page matches your lease category—verify.

💰

Security deposits

For many covered tenancies, strict timelines and itemization rules apply; bad-faith retention can trigger statutory penalties—read §92.103.

Serious repairs

Retaliation

Security deposits

What is common under Chapter 92 (when it applies)

  • No statewide dollar cap on how large a deposit can be (market practice still matters for competitiveness).
  • Return timeline: landlords commonly must return the deposit within 30 days after the tenant surrenders possession, with an itemized list of deductions when any amount is withheld—see §92.103 for the exact text and tenant forwarding-address requirements tied to remedies.
  • Wear and tear: landlords should not treat ordinary wear and tear as “damage.” Disputes often turn on photos, move-in condition reports, and invoices.

Document your forwarding address
Use email or letter with proof of delivery. Tenant remedies for wrongful withholding are sensitive to whether you followed statutory notice steps—don’t rely on verbal handoffs alone.

Habitability and “materially affects health or safety”

Texas uses a focused standard for many repair remedies: conditions that materially affect the physical health or safety of an ordinary tenant (see §92.052). That is narrower than “everything I want upgraded.”

Examples that often trigger serious repair discussions

  • Sewage backup, broken toilets, or no running water
  • Raw electrical hazards or gas leaks reported in writing
  • Meaningful roof leaks causing active interior water intrusion
  • Severe winter heat loss or dangerous summer cooling failures when conditions are extreme (fact-specific)

What often does not fit the same remedy path

  • Purely cosmetic issues
  • Minor appliance annoyances
  • Problems caused by tenant misuse

Repair rights: follow the statute’s sequence

If you skip steps, you can lose remedies—even if the landlord is slow. This is why “just stop paying rent” is dangerous in Texas unless counsel confirms a lawful strategy for your facts.

  • 1
    Written notice to the landlord at the address required by your lease (or statutory address rules). Keep a copy.
    Email may be OK if that is how your landlord actually conducts business—but confirm what your lease says.
  • 2
    Reasonable time to cure. Courts look at urgency; emergencies are different from slow drips.
    Do not invent a fixed “7 days” rule as universal—treat timing as fact-specific and document everything.
  • 3
    Follow-up notice when the statute requires a second notice before certain remedies.
    Read §92.056 and related sections carefully (or have legal aid walk you through them).
  • 4
    Remedies if the landlord still fails may include termination, repair-and-deduct (strict limits), or suit—each has prerequisites.
    Rent generally must be current for many remedies—get individualized advice before acting.

Do not withhold rent on your own theory
Non-payment eviction cases move quickly. If you believe you have repair-based defenses, you want evidence and counsel—not a guess.

Eviction process (orientation)

Texas Justice Court eviction practice is deadline-heavy. Treat any citation as urgent.

1

Notice

Written notice to vacate

For rent default, landlords typically must deliver a notice to vacate before suit. Delivery rules and timing matter; leases may extend notice beyond the statutory minimum.

2

Filing

Eviction petition

If the tenant does not vacate or resolve the default, the landlord files in the Justice Court with jurisdiction.

3

Hearing

Appear or risk default

Hearing dates depend on citation and local court rules. Bring evidence (ledger, receipts, photos, communications) and defenses you are prepared to prove.

4

Judgment / appeal

Appeals have strict rules

Appeals may require paying rent into the court registry or posting a bond in some situations. This is not DIY-friendly—get help immediately.

5

Writ

Writ of possession

If a landlord prevails and the process runs its course, a writ can be requested and executed by a constable—still not a landlord “self-help” removal.

For landlord-side procedure context, see How to evict a tenant: state-by-state guide — but tenants should rely on tenant resources and counsel for defenses.

What landlords should not do (illegal “self-help”)

Texas law targets lockouts, utility interruption to force move-out, and certain property removals intended to deny possession—see §92.0081 and related sections. Remedies can include statutory damages (commonly discussed as one month’s rent plus $1,000 in many post-2008 leases, subject to offsets and statute text), actual damages, costs, and attorney’s fees in qualifying suits.

Retaliation: Chapter 92 also contains anti-retaliation protections tied to specific protected activities and timing. Retaliation claims are fact-heavy—document a timeline.

Lease termination: special cases

  • Military (SCRA): federal protections may allow lease termination on qualifying orders—use the official process and documentation.
  • Family violence / sexual assault: Texas provides a statutory early-termination path for qualifying tenants with required documentation—see §92.016.
  • Landlord failure to repair (serious conditions): termination may be available only after the statutory process is satisfied—do not assume.
  • Breaking a lease without a statutory basis: you may owe rent through mitigation/reletting rules; landlords generally must try to mitigate, but you should not assume outcomes.

State comparisons go stale fast. Texas is often described as faster-moving on non-payment cases than many states, with no statewide rent caps for most private housing.

Frequently asked questions

Can a landlord evict you without notice in Texas?

Generally no. For typical non-payment cases, landlords must give a written notice to vacate before filing an eviction suit, and many leases use a short notice period often discussed as three days for rent default (your lease may require longer). Eviction still requires a court process. Lockouts, utility shutoffs to force you out, or removing your belongings without a court order are serious violations with statutory remedies—read the current Property Code and talk to legal aid or a Texas attorney if it happens to you.

How long does a landlord have to return a security deposit in Texas?

For many residential tenancies covered by Chapter 92, landlords must return a security deposit within 30 days after the tenant surrenders possession, with an itemized list of deductions when money is withheld. Bad-faith retention can carry penalties under the statute, but exemptions exist for certain small landlords. Provide a written forwarding address as required for tenant remedies and read §92.103 on Texas Law Help or with counsel.

What can a landlord deduct from my security deposit in Texas?

When Chapter 92 deposit rules apply, landlords generally may withhold amounts tied to unpaid rent and damages beyond normal wear and tear, with itemization. “Wear and tear” vs. “damage” fights depend on evidence. If you dispute deductions, consider legal aid or small-claims options with counsel.

Use the law + your records

Texas rewards documentation. If your landlord participates in PTI, you can combine statutory notices with a clearer paper trail for payments and maintenance.
