NSPIRE's Affirmative Habitability Requirements:
What Your Unit Must Have — Not Just What Can't Be Broken.

Most NSPIRE standards identify defects — things that can't be broken. The Affirmative Habitability Requirements under 24 CFR 5.703(d) work the other way: they define what a unit must affirmatively have to be a home. Food storage. A working cooking appliance. A functional sink. A private bathroom. Missing any is a Moderate defect.

By Perfect Tenant Innovation  ·  September 2026  ·  8 min read

Source: 24 CFR 5.703(d) · HUD NSPIRE HCV/PBV Inspection Checklist (OMB 2577-0289) · HUD REAC NSPIRE Standards

Nearly every NSPIRE standard covered in this series so far shares a common structure: it identifies a defect. A smoke alarm that's missing. An outlet that isn't grounded. Mold that's growing. A temperature that's too low. The inspector looks at something that exists in the unit and evaluates whether it's broken, hazardous, or deteriorated.

The Affirmative Habitability Requirements work in the opposite direction. Instead of asking "is this component broken?", they ask "does this unit have the things a home needs to be a home?" Established under 24 CFR 5.703(d), these requirements define the affirmative features a dwelling unit must possess to be considered habitable — regardless of whether everything present is in good repair. A unit could have zero defects in everything it contains and still fail NSPIRE if it's missing a required habitability feature entirely.

This is a subtle but important distinction, and it's one that catches landlords who think about inspection purely in terms of fixing what's broken. You can't fix your way to habitability compliance if the unit is missing a required feature altogether. This post explains what those affirmative requirements are, why they exist, and how to make sure every unit in your portfolio has what NSPIRE says a home must have.

The key conceptual difference

Most NSPIRE standards
"Is this component broken or hazardous?"
The inspector evaluates something that exists in the unit. A smoke alarm, an outlet, a heating system, a painted surface. The question is whether it's functioning, safe, and in good repair. The defect is a problem with something present.
Affirmative Habitability Requirements
"Does this unit have what a home requires?"
The inspector evaluates whether the unit possesses required features at all. Food storage, a cooking appliance, a functional sink, private bathroom facilities. The defect is the absence of something required — not the disrepair of something present.
You cannot repair your way out of a missing feature This is what makes the Affirmative Habitability Requirements distinct in a landlord's compliance planning. If an outlet is broken, you fix the outlet. If a unit has no cooking appliance at all, there's nothing to fix — you have to add the missing feature. A pre-inspection walkthrough focused only on "what's broken" will miss an affirmative habitability gap entirely, because the gap isn't a broken thing. It's an absent thing.

The Affirmative Habitability Requirements

The requirements under 24 CFR 5.703(d) center on the essential functions a dwelling must support: preparing and storing food, washing, and using bathroom facilities in private. Here are the core affirmative features NSPIRE requires a unit to have.

🍳
A working cooking appliance
The unit must have a functional appliance for cooking food — typically a stove or range with working burners and, where applicable, a working oven. The appliance must be present and operational. A missing cooking appliance, or one that is present but inoperable, means the unit cannot support the basic function of preparing hot meals.
Missing or inoperable = Moderate defect · 30-day correction
Under 24 CFR 5.703(d) affirmative habitability provisions
❄️
Adequate food storage
The unit must have adequate space and means for storing food — which in practice centers on a working refrigerator for perishable food storage, along with space for non-perishable storage. A missing refrigerator, or a refrigerator that doesn't maintain safe cold temperatures, undermines the unit's ability to store food safely.
Missing refrigerator = Moderate defect · 30-day correction
Under 24 CFR 5.703(d) affirmative habitability provisions
🔪
A food preparation area
The unit must have space for preparing food — a counter or equivalent surface where meals can be assembled and prepared. This is the workspace between storage and cooking. A kitchen with no usable preparation surface fails to provide a basic habitability function even if it has a stove and refrigerator.
Missing prep area = Moderate defect · 30-day correction
Under 24 CFR 5.703(d) affirmative habitability provisions
🚰
A functional kitchen sink
The unit must have a working kitchen sink with hot and cold running water and proper drainage. The kitchen sink is essential for food preparation, cleaning, and sanitation. A missing kitchen sink — or one without running water or with non-functional drainage — is an affirmative habitability failure.
Missing or non-functional = Moderate defect · 30-day correction
Under 24 CFR 5.703(d) affirmative habitability provisions
🚽
A toilet, usable in private
The unit must have a working toilet located in a space where it can be used in private. Both elements matter: the toilet must function, and it must be in a location that provides privacy. A toilet that works but is in a space that can't be closed off for privacy fails the affirmative requirement.
Missing, non-functional, or no privacy = Moderate defect · 30-day correction
Under 24 CFR 5.703(d) affirmative habitability provisions
🛁
A bathtub or shower, usable in private
The unit must have a working bathtub or shower with hot and cold running water, located in a space where it can be used in private. Like the toilet, both function and privacy are required. A shower that works but can't be used privately does not satisfy the affirmative habitability standard.
Missing, non-functional, or no privacy = Moderate defect · 30-day correction
Under 24 CFR 5.703(d) affirmative habitability provisions
🚿
A bathroom sink (lavatory)
The unit must have a working bathroom sink — separate from the kitchen sink — with hot and cold running water and proper drainage, for handwashing and personal hygiene. A bathroom with a toilet and shower but no functional sink is missing a required habitability component.
Missing or non-functional = Moderate defect · 30-day correction
Under 24 CFR 5.703(d) affirmative habitability provisions

The privacy requirement — a distinctive affirmative standard

🔒 Toilet and bathing facilities must be usable in private
Function alone isn't enough. The bathroom must provide privacy — a distinctive affirmative requirement most landlords have never had to think about explicitly.
The privacy requirement is one of the more distinctive elements of NSPIRE's affirmative habitability provisions. It's not asking whether the toilet flushes or the shower runs — it's asking whether the tenant can use these facilities with privacy. This means the bathroom space must be able to be closed off — a door that closes and provides visual privacy. A toilet in an alcove with no door, a shower in an open area, or bathroom facilities that can only be accessed by passing through another occupant's private space can all raise privacy concerns under this standard. For most conventional units this is automatically satisfied by a standard bathroom with a door. But in irregular unit configurations — converted spaces, single-room occupancy conversions, unusual floor plans — the privacy requirement can surface as a real deficiency. It's worth confirming that every bathroom in your portfolio can genuinely be used in private.

Who provides what — landlord vs. tenant responsibility

A nuance worth understanding: the required function must be present, regardless of who supplies it
For some affirmative habitability features — particularly appliances like the refrigerator and cooking appliance — whether the landlord or the tenant is responsible for providing the item can depend on the lease terms and the PHA's policies. Some leases have the landlord provide all appliances; others allow tenants to provide their own refrigerator or stove. NSPIRE's concern is with the presence and function of the required feature at inspection, not with who owns it. However, from a compliance-risk standpoint, the landlord's HCV contract is what absorbs the deficiency if the feature is missing at inspection. This means that even where a tenant is responsible for providing an appliance, the landlord has a strong interest in confirming the required features are present and functional before the inspection — because it's the landlord's contract on the line. Clarify appliance responsibility in the lease, and verify presence before every inspection regardless of who's responsible.
A tenant who removes their own appliance can create a landlord deficiency If a lease has the tenant provide their own refrigerator, and the tenant moves it out or it stops working, the unit now lacks a required affirmative habitability feature — and it's the landlord's HCV contract that shows the deficiency at inspection. This is structurally similar to the unvented heater problem: a tenant action creates a compliance exposure for the landlord. The defense is the same: know what's in your units, clarify responsibilities in writing, and verify required features are present before every inspection.

The affirmative habitability pre-inspection checklist

Run this checklist in every unit before every NSPIRE inspection. Unlike a defect checklist, this one confirms presence and function of required features — the things that must be there, working, and (for bathroom facilities) private.

A cooking appliance (stove/range) is present and every burner and the oven function
A refrigerator is present and maintains safe cold temperatures for food storage
There is a usable food preparation surface (counter or equivalent) in the kitchen
The kitchen sink works — hot and cold running water, proper drainage, no leaks
The toilet functions properly and is in a space that can be used in private
The bathtub or shower works — hot and cold running water — and can be used in private
The bathroom sink works — hot and cold running water, proper drainage
Every bathroom door closes and provides genuine visual privacy

Action steps: ensuring affirmative habitability compliance

1
Inventory the required features in every unit — confirm presence, not just condition
Walk every unit specifically to confirm that all required affirmative habitability features are present: cooking appliance, refrigerator, food prep surface, kitchen sink, toilet, tub/shower, bathroom sink. This is a presence inventory, distinct from a defect inspection. Note any unit where a required feature is absent — that's a gap you fill, not a defect you fix.
2
Clarify appliance responsibility in every lease
Make explicit in writing whether the landlord or tenant provides the refrigerator and cooking appliance. Ambiguity here creates compliance risk — if no one is clearly responsible and the appliance goes missing, it's your HCV contract that absorbs the deficiency. Clear lease language protects both parties and clarifies who must act when a required appliance needs replacement.
3
Verify bathroom privacy in every unit — especially unconventional layouts
Confirm that every toilet and bathing facility can genuinely be used in private — a door that closes and provides visual privacy. This is automatic in standard units but can be a real issue in converted spaces, unusual floor plans, or units where a bathroom door has been removed or never existed. Address any privacy gap before inspection.
4
Test all water functions at the fixtures — hot and cold, supply and drainage
For every required sink, tub, and shower, confirm hot and cold running water flows and drains properly. Affirmative habitability requires not just the fixture's presence but its function. Run the water, check the drainage, confirm the hot water reaches the fixture. A present-but-non-functional fixture fails the affirmative requirement just as a missing one does.
5
Confirm required features are present at every turnover and before every inspection
Build affirmative habitability verification into your turnover process and your pre-inspection routine. A unit that passed with all features present last year could be missing a tenant-provided appliance this year. Because the required features can change with tenancy — especially tenant-provided appliances — presence verification is an ongoing task, not a one-time confirmation.
Affirmative habitability is NSPIRE stating the obvious — that a home must function as a home There's something clarifying about the Affirmative Habitability Requirements. Stripped of technical severity tiers and correction timeframes, they simply state what everyone already knows a home requires: a place to cook, a place to keep food cold, a place to prepare a meal, running water to clean with, and a bathroom you can use in private. NSPIRE codifies these not because they're controversial but because a housing program serving vulnerable families needs an enforceable floor beneath which no unit can fall. For any landlord genuinely providing decent housing, these requirements are already met. The value of knowing them explicitly is making sure that a gap — a removed appliance, a missing prep surface, a privacy issue in an odd layout — never slips through unnoticed.
