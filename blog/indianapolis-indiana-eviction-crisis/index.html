One landlord. One year. Two hundred and eighty-five evictions.

That number — documented by the Fair Housing Center of Central Indiana in their 2026 eviction series — is not the story of a housing crisis in the abstract. It is the story of a specific company, BSG Enterprises, filing 137 eviction cases from a single 204-unit complex called Heather Ridge Apartments in one year. That's more than two eviction filings for every three apartments in the building.

That one property, that one landlord, is the concentrated version of what Indianapolis's eviction data shows at scale. A 14% eviction filing rate — sixth in the nation. 116,489 eviction cases in Marion County between January 2021 and September 2025. And a finding from Indiana University's Polis Center that just 100 properties across the city account for one-third of all habitability and code violations. The same landlords filing the most evictions are managing the worst properties.

Why Indianapolis's eviction rate is this high: four structural drivers

1
Concentrated corporate landlord power using eviction as routine management
The FHCCI data reveals a pattern that Eviction Lab researchers have documented in every high-eviction city: a small number of landlords filing a disproportionate share of total evictions. When a single landlord files 285 evictions in a year — and 137 from one complex — eviction is not a last resort. It is a management strategy. Corporate landlords optimizing for cash flow have learned that the threat of eviction filing is an efficient tool for extracting payment, even when the underlying habitability of the property is documented as substandard.
2
Indiana's 10-day notice and limited tenant protections
Indiana requires landlords to give tenants 10 days to pay or vacate before filing for eviction for non-payment — slightly longer than the 3-to-5-day windows in other high-eviction states, but still tight for a household navigating an income disruption. Indiana's landlord-tenant law offers limited tenant protections: no rent control, no source-of-income protection requiring acceptance of vouchers, and no guaranteed right to counsel in eviction proceedings. The legal framework is designed to facilitate landlord rights more than tenant stability.
3
Substandard housing conditions concentrated in high-eviction properties
The Indiana University Polis Center finding — that 100 properties account for one-third of all habitability and code violations — means the renters most at risk of eviction are also living in the city's worst housing. Landlords filing the most evictions are managing properties with documented maintenance failures. Tenants who stop paying rent because their unit is uninhabitable face eviction anyway, in a legal system that — without representation — rarely pauses to assess whether the landlord's own obligations were met.
4
Post-moratorium filing surge that never fully receded
Marion County recorded 116,489 eviction cases between January 2021 and September 2025 — a period that started with pandemic-era moratoriums lifting and eviction courts resuming at volume. The backlog from 2020–2021 combined with rising rents and stagnant lower-income wages produced a sustained filing rate that, unlike some other cities, has not returned to pre-pandemic baseline. Indiana's diversion program — which allows cases to be paused 90 days for payment arrangements — exists but requires tenant initiative to access, and many renters don't know it's available.
Indiana's diversion option — most renters don't know it exists Indiana courts allow tenants and landlords who agree to a pre-eviction diversion to have a case marked confidential and paused for 90 days while they pursue assistance or a payment arrangement. If that case ultimately resolves, the record can be sealed. Most renters facing eviction in Indianapolis never access this option because they don't know it exists or don't have representation to help them navigate it. If you receive an eviction summons in Indianapolis, ask immediately about diversion options.

What would change this — and what you can do now

Researchers and advocates have identified the interventions that reduce high-volume eviction filing: requiring right to counsel in eviction court (which dramatically reduces default judgments), automatically informing tenants of diversion options, raising filing fees to discourage routine eviction-as-management, and strengthening habitability enforcement so landlords filing evictions can be countered with documented code violations. Some of these are available to Indianapolis through city ordinance. Others require state action.
