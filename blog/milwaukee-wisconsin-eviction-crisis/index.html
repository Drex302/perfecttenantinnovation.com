In 2008 and 2009, a Princeton sociologist named Matthew Desmond moved into a trailer park on Milwaukee's North Side and then into a rooming house on the same side of the city. He lived alongside the people he was studying — tenants facing eviction, landlords filing against them, a city where Black renters were being displaced at a rate he had never documented anywhere else. The book that came out of that fieldwork, Evicted: Poverty and Profit in the American City, won the Pulitzer Prize and became the foundational text of America's understanding of its eviction crisis.

The database Desmond built afterward — Princeton University's Eviction Lab, which now tracks 48 cities and 10 states — confirmed in data what he had observed in Milwaukee in person: that this city has a racial eviction disparity unlike any other place in the country. In 2025, that disparity reached its starkest documented level.

36% of Milwaukee's renters are Black. 66% of eviction filings were against Black renters. A 30-percentage-point gap — the single most extreme racial disparity in the Eviction Lab's entire 48-city tracking network. The researcher who built the database that measured it first saw it, up close, on the North Side of this city.

📚 Why Milwaukee is where this story began
Matthew Desmond chose Milwaukee for the fieldwork that became Evicted because it was, at the time, one of the most thoroughly documented eviction cities in the country — and one of the most racially segregated. The book followed real Milwaukee tenants through the eviction process, lived with them in the properties being litigated, and named the landlords filing the cases. When Desmond later built the Eviction Lab to scale those observations to every tracked city in America, Milwaukee's numbers remained among the most extreme in the dataset. The book that explained the eviction crisis to the country was written in the city that, by 2025, had the worst racial disparity in the nation's eviction data.

To understand what that gap means in practice: in a city where Black renters are 36% of the renter population, a proportional eviction system would produce roughly 36% of filings against Black tenants. Instead, nearly twice that share — 66% — are being filed against Black renters. The other 34% of renters — predominantly white — face eviction at rates far below their population share.

More than two-thirds of eviction filings in Milwaukee land in majority-Black neighborhoods, according to Eviction Lab data. The city's North Side — the same North Side where Desmond conducted his fieldwork — carries the heaviest concentration of eviction activity.

"The disparity highlights Milwaukee's status as the nation's most segregated metropolitan area, home to stark racial inequality created and maintained by decades of neglect from business and political leaders."

Why Milwaukee is the most segregated city in America — and why that explains the eviction data

These two facts — Milwaukee's status as the most segregated metropolitan area in the United States, and its status as the city with the worst racial eviction disparity in the Eviction Lab dataset — are not coincidental. They are the same structural history expressed in two different data points.

The Brookings Institution found that 79.8% of Black Milwaukeeans would need to move to different neighborhoods before the city could be considered fully integrated. For context: Los Angeles, ranked 10th most segregated, has a comparable figure of only 66.8%. Milwaukee's segregation is more extreme than cities twice its size. Understanding how it got there explains why 66% of eviction filings fall on 36% of its renters.

1930s
Federal redlining maps draw Milwaukee's racial geography
The Home Owners' Loan Corporation grades Milwaukee's neighborhoods by race. Black communities — concentrated in Bronzeville on the North Side — are designated hazardous investment risks. White families access FHA mortgage financing and suburban homeownership. Black families are locked into rental housing in redlined zones. The homeownership gap begins compounding on day one.
1940s–60s
Racial covenants and suburban white flight entrench segregation
Racially restrictive covenants — recently mapped by a University of Wisconsin project — locked Black residents out of Wauwatosa, Greenfield, and the suburbs surrounding Milwaukee. As white families left the city for FHA-financed suburban homes, Black families remained in the urban rental market, surrounded by suburbs that were legally closed to them. Milwaukee's metropolitan segregation calcified in this period.
1960s–80s
Manufacturing collapse removes the economic floor from Black neighborhoods
Milwaukee's North Side Black community had built economic stability through manufacturing employment — a path to middle-class income that didn't require homeownership or credit access. The collapse of Milwaukee's industrial base in the 1970s and 1980s removed that floor. Neighborhoods that had sustained working-class Black families became high-poverty, high-rental-burden communities. Eviction filing rates followed.
1990s–2010s
Disinvestment, gentrification, and the landlord class consolidates
As Desmond documented in Evicted, Milwaukee's rental market in majority-Black North Side neighborhoods became a profitable extraction system for a class of landlords who charged rents above market rate for substandard housing, knowing tenants had nowhere else to go in a hypersegregated city. The eviction filing was a management tool, not a last resort.
2025
36% of renters. 66% of eviction filings.
Black homeownership in Milwaukee remains below 30% — compared to nearly 60% for white residents. The wealth-building that homeownership provided to white Milwaukee families for three generations has bypassed Black Milwaukee families. The result is a majority-renter Black population concentrated in the most eviction-intensive neighborhoods of the most segregated city in America.

The homeownership gap that makes the eviction data inevitable

The homeownership rate in Milwaukee is not an abstraction. It is the direct mechanism connecting the city's segregation history to its eviction crisis. Black families who were locked out of homeownership by redlining, racial covenants, and discriminatory mortgage practices became renters by policy. Their children became renters. Their grandchildren became renters. Three generations of compulsory rental status in a city where eviction filing is concentrated on renters in majority-Black neighborhoods is how you produce a 30-point racial eviction gap.

What the homeownership gap means for eviction risk A homeowner who misses a mortgage payment faces foreclosure — a process that typically takes months and includes multiple legal protections. A renter who misses rent faces eviction — a process that in Wisconsin can result in a filing within days. When one racial group is disproportionately concentrated in the renter class by decades of policy, that group absorbs a disproportionate share of eviction filings. The 30-point gap in Milwaukee's eviction data is not a mystery. It is arithmetic applied to history.

What drives Milwaukee's eviction filing rate: four current factors

1
Wisconsin's 5-day pay-or-quit notice and limited tenant protections
Wisconsin requires five days' notice before a landlord can file for eviction for non-payment — brief enough that a renter dealing with a delayed paycheck or an unexpected expense has minimal time to resolve the gap. Wisconsin's landlord-tenant law offers no source-of-income protection requiring acceptance of vouchers, no right to counsel in eviction proceedings, and no rent control. The legal architecture gives landlords efficient filing access and tenants minimal structural protection.
2
Hypersegregation concentrating eviction pressure in specific neighborhoods
Because Milwaukee is the most segregated metropolitan area in the United States, eviction pressure concentrates geographically in a way that amplifies racial disparities. The North Side neighborhoods where Black renters are most concentrated are the same neighborhoods where eviction filing rates are highest, landlord disinvestment is most documented, and alternative housing options are most limited. Segregation doesn't just correlate with eviction — it determines where eviction filing clusters.
3
Eviction used as property management — not as last resort
Milwaukee housing advocates, including MATU (Metropolitan Tenants' Alliance of Milwaukee), have documented a pattern of landlords using eviction filing as a routine management tool — filing for minor arrears, for lease technicalities, and as retaliation for maintenance complaints — rather than reserving it for genuine non-payment situations. "Tenants are being evicted at a stunning rate and are dealing with illegal eviction, retaliation, intimidation, and unlivable conditions," MATU told the Wisconsin Examiner. In the absence of strong tenant protections, filing is low-cost and nearly always successful.
4
Rising rents in a historically low-wage rental market
Milwaukee's rental market has experienced consistent rent increases against a backdrop of stagnant wages in the service and healthcare sectors that employ a large share of North Side residents. Black renters burdened by rising rents and stagnant wages have little margin for error — a single income disruption is frequently enough to trigger the filing process in a city where 36% of the renter population absorbs 66% of its eviction filings.

What would change this — and what you can do while it doesn't

Housing advocates and researchers have identified the interventions that would reduce Milwaukee's eviction rate and its racial disparity: universal access to legal representation in eviction court, stronger habitability enforcement so landlords filing evictions can be counter-challenged on property conditions, source-of-income protections that prevent landlords from discriminating against voucher holders, and longer notice periods that give renters a realistic window to access emergency assistance.

On the structural side, closing Milwaukee's homeownership gap through expanded access to down payment assistance, BIPOC-focused community development financial institutions, and anti-discrimination enforcement in mortgage lending would — over time — shift the racial composition of the renter class and reduce the concentration of eviction risk.

These are generational changes. Milwaukee renters do not have a generation to wait.
